# Does online anonymity do more harm than good — and is less of it even on the table?

*Status: open · last touched 2026-10-03 · sources checked 2026-10-03*

## The question

A video about VPNs set this off. The pitch in that genre is always the same — install this, become invisible — and what struck me was not whether the product delivers, but that I had no settled view on whether I wanted it to. Anonymity online is something I have always treated as a feature of the landscape rather than as something anyone chose, and when I tried to say whether it was good that we have it, I found I leaned one way without being able to say why.

So: **does online anonymity do more harm than good — and if we decided it did, could we actually have less of it?**

The second half is not a rhetorical flourish. Most of what gets said about anonymity assumes it is a setting someone could turn down, and that assumption deserves to be checked before the moral arithmetic starts, because an ethical argument about an option nobody has is a waste of everyone's time.

### Three rungs, routinely confused

"Anonymity" covers three different conditions, and a great many arguments about it are arguments in which each side has a different one in mind.

- **Pseudonymity.** I write as someone who is not my legal name, but the platform knows who I am — it has my phone number, my payment card, the addresses I connected from. The audience cannot identify me; a court order can. This is the condition most people are actually in when they think they are anonymous.
- **Platform-blind anonymity.** Nobody at the platform knows either: no account tied to anything that identifies me. But the network still records that this device spoke to that server at that moment, so the link exists for anyone able to observe both ends.
- **Network-blind anonymity.** Neither the platform nor an observer on the wire can connect the content to the person. This is what onion routing and mixnets are built for, and it is the only rung on which "anonymous" means what the word is usually taken to mean.

The rungs matter because the costs and the benefits are not distributed evenly across them. A troll needs only the first. A dissident under a government that can subpoena the platform needs the third. If the harms of anonymity concentrate on one rung and the benefits on another, then the question "should anonymity be available" has no single answer, and the interesting work is in locating the line rather than in defending either end of it.

### Three things anonymity is not

**Privacy** is about what is seen; anonymity is about who did it. I can be fully identified and still have my messages private, and I can be anonymous while everything I write is in the open — in fact that is the usual case, since the point of anonymous speech is that it be read.

**Encryption** protects content, not authorship. It hides what I said from everyone except the recipient, while leaving the fact that I said something to someone entirely visible. Metadata is the part that identifies, and encryption does not touch it.

**Impunity** is what anonymity gets accused of being, and it is not the same thing. Anonymity raises the price of finding out who acted; it does not repeal the rule that was broken, and it does not make the act undiscoverable. The distinction matters for the argument that follows, because "harder to prosecute" and "impossible to prosecute" support very different conclusions.

### My starting position

Written down before I went looking, so it can be seen afterwards whether it survived: **online anonymity is mildly bad.** Not catastrophic, not a thing I would tear out at any cost, but on balance a loss. Three reasons, in the order they occurred to me:

1. **It hides illegal activity, and that activity hurts innocent people.** This is the reason I hold most firmly. Whatever else anonymity does, it is the operating condition for markets and exchanges whose victims never consented to any of it.
2. **It enables the spread of disinformation.** Claims that would die under a name survive when nobody has to own them.
3. **It enables bot farms.** Inauthentic accounts at scale are possible because accounts need not correspond to people.

I am aware this is the common position rather than a worked-out one, which is the reason for writing the note rather than for trusting the position. What I want to know is which of those three reasons survives contact with the evidence, and whether what is left supports the conclusion I started with or a different one.

## Conjectures

- **A — Net harm.** The balance is negative. Anonymity is the operating condition for categories of harm whose victims are picked at random and never consented to anything: markets in material produced by abusing children, extortion operations that take hospitals offline, fraud run at industrial scale. Against that sit benefits that are real but diffuse, and largely enjoyed by people who had other options. A world with less anonymity online would be a better world — and the fact that this is also the intuitive position is not by itself an argument against it. *(This is my starting position, stated here in its strongest form rather than its most common one.)*
- **B — Shield of the weak.** Anonymity is the necessary condition for speech by people with something to lose: whistleblowers, dissidents, people reporting an abuser they live with, journalistic sources, anyone persecuted for an identity they cannot put down. Every mechanism that strips anonymity strips it from these people first, because they are the ones for whom being identified carries a cost. The harms are the price of there being any opposition to power at all.
- **C — Not a dial.** The question "should anonymity be available" is malformed, because anonymity is not a setting anyone controls. Either the technical possibility of network-blind communication exists — in which case it cannot be withdrawn from bad actors in particular — or it does not, in which case the people under threat do not have it either. There is no middle position on the dial. There is only the question of who holds the key.
- **D — The middle rung.** Most of the benefit comes from pseudonymity, where a person can still be found by due process, and most of the harm requires network-blind anonymity, where nobody can be found at all. The answer is therefore neither pole but a shift of one rung: let the name be optional, keep the trail.
- **E — Who holds the key.** The real question is not how much anonymity, but who may break it, on what grounds, and under whose supervision. Every proposal to end anonymity is in substance a proposal to hand someone a power, so the balance cannot be computed without knowing who that someone is and what restrains them.

## Refutations & tensions

The three reasons behind A do not hold up equally well, and the differences between them turned out to be the most informative thing in this note. I take them in the order I first thought of them, which is roughly the reverse of their strength.

### 1. Illegality: the firmest leg, and what it actually claims

The strong version of this leg is not that anonymity correlates with crime. It is that network-blind anonymity is the *operating condition* for particular markets — ransomware infrastructure that has to stay reachable while remaining unlocatable, and hosting for material produced by abusing children — and that the victims of those markets are selected at random and consent to nothing. Europol's threat assessments put the second of those plainly: Tor and its hidden services "facilitate practically untraceable sexual exploitation of children by allowing the exchange of images anonymously", and the reports describe anonymisation chains — traffic routed through successive VPN servers and Tor nodes — as a technique that complicates attribution and delays investigation, against a landscape of more than 120 active ransomware brands in 2025 *(as of 2026-10)*. So the leg is not a hunch; the agencies that investigate this say the same thing.

**Two objections to it, and I think they bite.** The first is that as I stated the leg above, it is close to unfalsifiable: I said it needs only that removing the anonymity would remove the market *in its current form*, which no observation can contradict, since any market with its anonymity removed is by definition no longer in that form. Framed that way the leg is true and does no work. To do work it has to claim something about quantity — that the harm is larger with anonymity than without — and that is a claim about displacement, not about operating conditions.

The second objection is what the displacement evidence shows. Australian Institute of Criminology research on darknet market seizures finds exactly the pattern that makes the quantity claim hard: vendors and buyers migrate rapidly between marketplaces, closures produce dispersal and substitution rather than disappearance, and the authors' conclusion is that market closures by law enforcement are unlikely to change public consumption of illicit drugs because new markets emerge to meet the existing demand. The honest qualification is that it is not nothing — seizures did reduce opioid and fentanyl availability and raise prices, though only once several major markets had fallen rather than after any single one. Which means the leg survives as "anonymity lowers the cost and raises the convenience of these markets" and not as "anonymity is why the harm exists". The demand, and most of the harm, predate the channel.

What the evidence also unsettles is the step from the leg to "therefore anonymity defeats enforcement". The two largest darknet markets to be taken down were not reached by breaking the anonymity. The FBI's account of Silk Road is that its server was located after a misconfiguration briefly exposed its IP address — an account Ulbricht's own expert witness called implausible on the basis of the server's configuration files, and which independent researchers have read as parallel construction, so I report it as the Bureau's version rather than as settled fact. Either way the identification ran through ordinary mistakes: Ross Ulbricht had advertised the site under the handle `altoid` on public forums and then reused that handle to recruit developers, and the encryption key on the server carried the string `frosty@frosty`. AlphaBay fell further still from any technical break — its administrator's personal email address, `pimp_alex_91@hotmail.com`, had been sitting in the header of the welcome message the site sent every new user, and that address led to a PayPal account, to forums where he had posted under his own name, and from there to his real identity and his arrest in Bangkok in 2017.

So anonymity raised the price of the investigation without closing it. That is a weaker claim than the one usually made on this leg, and it cuts both ways: it means enforcement is possible, and it also means enforcement that succeeds does so by waiting for human error rather than by having a key. Both halves matter later, when E asks what a key would be worth.

### 2. Disinformation: the leg that shifted under me

Here the shape of my reason was wrong, and the sourcing corrected it in a way I did not expect — not by showing anonymity matters less, but by showing I had been imagining the wrong mechanism.

I had pictured disinformation as diffuse: a mass of unaccountable accounts, each contributing a little, with anonymity as the enabling condition for all of them. Grinberg and colleagues, matching Twitter accounts to registered voters during the 2016 US election, found engagement concentrated instead, and the two figures are worth keeping apart because they measure different things: "Only 1% of individuals accounted for 80% of fake news source exposures, and 0.1% accounted for nearly 80% of fake news sources shared." The first is consumption; the second is propagation. It is the second that matters here — one account in a thousand doing most of the sharing is not a mass of unaccountable accounts, which is what my reason assumed. They also found that "for people across the political spectrum, most political news exposure still came from mainstream media outlets."

There is also a measurable channel for misinformation that is not anonymous at all, though it is measured more loosely than I first wrote. Mosleh and Rand built a tool for scoring Twitter users' exposure to misinformation from "elites" — public figures and organisations — using PolitiFact fact-checks to assign falsity scores to 816 of them, and found users' exposure scores "negatively correlated with the quality of news they share themselves". Two caveats I owe the reader: exposure there is inferred from whom a user chooses to follow rather than observed directly, and the correlation carries no temporal order, so this is a follow-graph proxy and not a measured pipeline. What it does establish is that a large channel of fact-checked falsehood runs through 816 named, identifiable actors.

**What I must not claim here, and originally intended to.** I expected to write that the most effective disinformation travels under real names, because a name lends authority an anonymous account cannot borrow. Neither of these papers shows that. Grinberg measures concentration, not anonymity; Mosleh and Rand measure an elite channel without comparing it to an anonymous one. Neither abstract discusses anonymity at all. What the two together support is narrower: that disinformation is concentrated rather than diffuse, and that a large identified channel exists. Whether named sources outperform anonymous ones per unit of reach is a question I did not find answered, and it stays open rather than getting asserted.

Even so, the leg has moved. "Anonymity enables disinformation" was doing work in my position that it cannot do: whatever is driving a phenomenon concentrated in a tenth of a percent of users, it is not the general availability of anonymity.

### 3. Bot farms: the leg that inverted

This is the reason I would have defended most casually, and it is the one that turned out to be backwards. Inauthentic accounts at scale do not need anonymity. They need *identity* — and they buy it.

The infrastructure is a market with published prices. Roozenbeek, Dek and van der Linden tracked real-time pricing for the SMS verifications used to create fake accounts across more than 500 platforms over twelve months to July 2025, and in much of the world the numbers are not deterrents: $0.08 per verification in Russia, $0.10 in the UK, $0.26 in the US, with per-platform averages of $0.08 for Meta, $0.10 for X and Instagram, $0.11 for TikTok and LinkedIn *(as of 2025-07)*. But the same table contains its own counter-evidence, which I should not have quoted only the cheap end of: verification runs $4.93 in Japan and $3.24 in Australia, and $0.89 to $1.02 on WhatsApp and Telegram. A sixtyfold spread is not a uniform eight cents, and the expensive cells are the ones that matter for C and D below. The industrial end of the business is larger: Europol's Operation SIMCARTEL dismantled a cybercrime-as-a-service network renting access to SIM farms, through which more than 49 million fake accounts are estimated to have been created, using SIM cards bought from almost 80 countries.

**And here is where the leg does not invert as cleanly as I wanted it to.** I reached for the Internet Research Agency as the case where identity is stolen rather than hidden, and the indictment does allege that its operatives used the Social Security numbers, addresses and dates of birth of real US persons, without their knowledge, to open four bank accounts and five PayPal accounts, with aggravated identity theft charged against four defendants. But read in context those stolen identities were the *payment and advertising plumbing*. The public-facing operation ran on personas that were invented, not impersonated — "Blacktivist", "Secured Borders", "United Muslims of America", "Heart of Texas" — and the indictment itself distinguishes further accounts opened "in the names of false and fictitious U.S. personas". A fictitious persona is the first rung of anonymity, which is precisely what my original reason was about. So the IRA case supports my starting reason more than it undercuts it, and I had it backwards.

What survives the correction is the verification market, which is the stronger evidence anyway: bought and rented verified identity at a published price, at a scale of tens of millions of accounts, is a business model in which identity checks appear as a line item rather than as an obstacle. And the qualification that comes with it is the finding I keep returning to: price is not irrelevant. The authors read higher SIM costs as "likely to suppress rates of malicious online activity". Stricter identity requirements, on this evidence, do not prevent the abuse; they price it. Whether that price is worth what it costs everyone else is a real question, and it is not the same question as whether anonymity should exist.

### Where that leaves A

One leg held, one moved, one inverted. The strongest form of my position is now narrower than the position I started with: it rests on network-blind anonymity being the operating condition for specific markets whose victims never consented, and it can no longer lean on disinformation or bot farms, because in those two the mechanism runs through identity — bought, stolen, or simply held by named elites — rather than through its absence. The honest summary of the shift is that anonymity makes some of what I objected to *cheaper*, and that it makes almost none of it *possible*.

### 4. How much harm, actually — and why the numbers will not come clean

Before the ethics, the size of the thing. This is where I expected to find a settled figure and found instead a cautionary tale about how such figures are made.

The number that circulates is that over 80% of requests to Tor hidden services go to abuse sites. It comes from Owen and Savage, who observed around 80,000 hidden services in the distributed hash table while running relays over six months, and who reported that abuse sites **and botnet command-and-control servers** were the most frequently requested — a co-equal second category that the popular version of the figure quietly drops, and which inflates the share attributed to abuse.

The Tor Project's reply is the more instructive document, and the objection in it is theirs rather than the authors' self-caveat — I had this attributed the wrong way round in an earlier draft. Hidden service traffic was put at roughly 1.5% of all Tor traffic *(as of 2014-12)*, so the 80% was never 80% of Tor. And there is a survivorship problem: "His graphs only show data about the sites that were still up many months later: so his data could either show a lot of people visiting abuse-related hidden services, or it could simply show that abuse-related hidden services are more long-lived than others." Without knowing how many sites vanished before the scan, the denominator is unknown. The underlying article is paywalled and I have not read it in full, which is itself a reason to lean on it lightly.

The best-grounded estimate I found works from the user side rather than the request side. Jardine, Lindner and Owenson, using data from Tor entry nodes, estimate that on an average country/day about 6.7% of Tor *users* connect to "Onion/Hidden Services that are disproportionately used for illicit purposes" *(as of 2020)*. Note what that does and does not license: it is a share of users, not a share of traffic, and the paper's phrasing is about services disproportionately used for illicit purposes rather than about illicit use itself. Even read carefully, it leaves the large majority of Tor users somewhere other than the part of the network the harms live in.

**And then the finding that cuts against where I was heading.** The same paper reports that the balance varies systematically with a country's political conditions, and not in the direction that flatters the liberal case: using Freedom House's classifications, illicit hidden-service use is *more* prevalent in "free" countries (~7.8%) than in "partially free" (~6.7%) or "not free" ones (~4.8%). The authors' own title says it: the potential harms of Tor cluster disproportionately in free countries. The reading is uncomfortable and I think it is correct. Anonymity's defensive value is highest where speech is punished, and its abuse value is highest where it is not — so for someone arguing about this from inside a democracy, the local balance is worse than the global one. B is a real argument, but it is an argument that mostly cashes out somewhere else, for someone else. That is a cost to my own conclusion, not to A's.

### 5. What a VPN actually does

This is where the video that started this gets its answer. A VPN does not remove traceability; it moves the trust. Without one, the party able to link my traffic to me is my internet provider. With one, it is the VPN operator — a company whose claim not to keep records is, from the outside, unfalsifiable marketing.

Two cases show what that exposure is worth, though both are older than they sound and both providers have since changed hands and submitted to audits *(as of 2026-10)*. IPVanish advertised a zero-logs policy; after a 2016 Homeland Security summons to its parent company it initially said it had no user data, then on a follow-up request produced connection logs with the subject's real name, email address, originating IP and the times of each connection. In a 2017 FBI cyberstalking investigation PureVPN, also advertising no logs, supplied connection timestamps and originating addresses. I first wrote that investigators called those records the key to the identification; reading the reporting on the affidavit more carefully, the suspect was already the prime suspect — a former roommate of the victim, identified through ordinary leads — and the VPN records corroborated a case that existed without them. The honest claim is narrower: a provider advertising no logs produced logs. What they were worth to that investigation is not established, and this is the weakest-supported pair of facts in the note.

So on the three rungs, a consumer VPN does not deliver network-blind anonymity and does not even reliably deliver platform-blind anonymity. It delivers pseudonymity with a single point of failure, and it relocates that point from a regulated utility to a company selected largely on the strength of its own advertising. For the hazards most buyers actually have in mind — a government, a serious adversary, a court order — the move is close to nil. Against an advertiser or an open Wi-Fi snoop it is real. The product is not a fraud; it is just sold against a threat model its buyers do not have.

### 6. Against C — and the objection I got wrong

This is the block I had to rewrite, because my first attempt refuted something C does not say.

The case I reached for was the UK's Online Safety Act, whose age-assurance duties took effect on 25 July 2025, requiring "highly effective age assurance" — facial age estimation, photo ID, credit card or bank checks — on services showing adult content. What followed is well documented: Proton reported a roughly 1,400% rise in UK sign-ups within hours of the duties biting and around 1,800% sustained over the following days, levels it compared to those it normally sees during civil unrest, while NordVPN reported around a 1,000% rise in purchases; VPN apps took four of the top five free slots in the UK App Store. Because the obligation attaches to UK IP addresses, appearing to be elsewhere suffices, and Ofcom has acknowledged it cannot block VPNs under the Act. A government consultation that ran from March to May 2026 asked, among other things, whether VPN providers should owe age-assurance duties and whether children's access to VPNs should be restricted *(as of 2026-10)*.

I wanted all that to show that middle settings exist and therefore that C is too strong. It does not. C is specifically about **network-blind** anonymity: either the technical possibility exists, in which case it cannot be withdrawn from bad actors in particular, or it does not. UK age assurance is an access control at the first or second rung; it never touched the third. And the outcome — routed around within hours, with the regulator conceding it cannot stop that — is exactly what C predicts. My counterexample was evidence *for* the conjecture I was attacking.

So does C stand? For network-blind anonymity I now think it mostly does, with one genuine qualification: that rung *can* be taken away, just not selectively. States that filter Tor at the network layer demonstrate it, and they demonstrate C's point in the process, since what they remove is removed from the dissident and the abuser together. The narrower true claim is that C holds at the third rung and says nothing about the first two, where settings do exist and are being legislated.

What those settings deliver is what the verification market already showed: identity requirements function as a **tax**, not a wall. The tax is not pointless — Roozenbeek and colleagues read higher SIM costs as likely to suppress malicious activity, and the sixtyfold spread between eight cents and Japan's $4.93 is the size of the lever. But it is levied on whoever will not route around it. The curious adolescent pays; the operation renting SIM farms through which tens of millions of accounts were created treats it as cost of goods.

### 7. Against B: necessary condition, or merely useful?

B claims anonymity is the *necessary* condition for speech by the exposed. The strongest objection is that the celebrated cases do not look like that. Whistleblowing in the democracies runs largely through identified channels with legal protection attached, and the famous disclosures went through journalists who knew exactly who their source was. Anonymity from the public is not the same as anonymity from everyone, and what those cases needed was a trusted intermediary, not an untraceable network. If anonymity is merely useful rather than necessary, B weighs less against A than its advocates suppose — and that conclusion is against the side I would instinctively defend.

What rescues part of B is a different kind of evidence, and I have to state its size more carefully than I first did. Penney, in the *Berkeley Technology Law Journal*, examined monthly views of 48 Wikipedia articles on terrorism-related topics the US Department of Homeland Security had listed as subjects it tracks, and reported "an immediate drop-off of over 30% of overall views" after the June 2013 Snowden revelations. That headline figure is not the effect attributable to surveillance, and Penney says so himself: excluding an outlier (the Hamas article, whose views were moving for unrelated reasons) gives "25% immediate drop-off", and all of English Wikipedia fell about 15% over the same month, so "even assuming that a full 15% of the drop off ... is simply reflective of background Wikipedia trends, 10% of the reduction in article views over June 2013 remains". Ten per cent is the conservative number, which Penney notes is twice the 5% fall in privacy-sensitive Google searches that Marthews and Tucker found over the same period. One further correction to my first draft: the privacy-sensitivity ratings came from a separate paid survey panel, not from the readers whose views were counted, and the measured quantity is page loads rather than people.

Even at 10%, the point stands and it is the point I need. Nobody in that data was blowing a whistle. They were reading an encyclopedia, legally, and some of them stopped because they believed they were being watched. That is anonymity doing work for ordinary people in a free country, work that has nothing to do with wrongdoing — and therefore work that no argument about whether whistleblowers strictly need anonymity can capture.

### 8. Against D: the middle rung has been tried, and it is where the trapdoor is

D is the position I found most attractive while drafting: keep the name optional, keep the trail. Two things are wrong with it.

The first is structural. Pseudonymity means someone holds the mapping from the handle to the person, and that someone can be breached, bought, subpoenaed, or simply sold to a new owner with different intentions. For the person B is about, "the platform knows who I am" is not a weaker form of protection, it is the absence of protection with a delay. Facebook's real-name policy is the worked example: enforcement fell on Native Americans, trans and drag performers, and domestic violence survivors, the policy was weaponised by trolls who mass-reported the accounts of people whose legal names were dangerous to them, and the company's 2014 apology did not stop the 2015 protest where the placards read "Facebook exposed me to my abuser".

The second is that the middle rung has been tried at scale, and the result is more interesting than the version I first wrote. South Korea required identity verification for posting comments from 2007 — though only on sites averaging more than 100,000 visitors a day, a threshold that matters for reading what happened next — and in August 2012 the Constitutional Court struck the requirement down unanimously, reasoning that after the system was introduced the volume of illegal postings had not declined significantly while users had migrated to overseas sites the rule could not reach.

**But the civility half of my claim was wrong, and peer-reviewed work I had not read says so.** Cho, Kim and Acquisti, analysing comments on Korean news sites across the policy change, found the identification requirement had "significant effects on reducing uninhibited behaviours at the aggregate level" — swearing and anti-normative posting fell substantially — while having no significant effect on any individual user's behavioural shift, with light commenters affected and heavy commenters not. So the real-name regime did buy a measure of civility; what it did not buy was a fall in illegal postings, and it came with offshore migration that the 100,000-visitor threshold made almost inevitable.

That is a more honest and less convenient finding than "it delivered nothing". It also splits the ledger in a way that matters for D: the middle rung works on the marginal, careless participant and not on the committed one — which is the same shape as the verification tax, arriving from a completely different direction. I earlier called this the most load-bearing evidence in the note; that was overstated, since the court's finding reaches me through a news report of its reasoning rather than through a measurement, and it is the Cho study that carries the measured part.

### 9. The visibility asymmetry

One structural reason to distrust my own intuition here, which applies equally to anyone else's. The harms of anonymity are *countable*: they have victims, case numbers, prosecutions, press coverage. The benefits are not merely hard to count, they are unobservable in principle. A person who spoke because they could not be identified produces no record of the alternative in which they stayed silent; the dissident who was never arrested, the woman who reported an abuser from an account he could not trace, the reader who looked something up without fearing a file — none of them generate an incident report. The ledger has one column filled in.

So any honest estimate of the balance is being made from evidence that systematically favours the conclusion that anonymity costs more than it returns. That is not an argument that anonymity is good. It is a reason to hold whatever conclusion I reach more loosely than the evidence superficially allows, and specifically a reason to discount the confidence of my own starting position rather than its content.

### 10. Who holds the key — and what the key is worth

E says the question was never how much anonymity but who may break it. Four findings above converge on this, which is why I now think E is doing more work than I gave it credit for.

Abolishing anonymity outright is rarely what gets proposed, and where it has been — South Korea legislated pseudonymous posting off its largest sites — it did not hold. What gets proposed is a mechanism: a verification requirement, a retention mandate, a lawful-access provision, a scanning obligation, an age-assurance duty. Each names a holder — a platform, a regulator, a ministry, a vendor — and hands them a capability that did not previously exist. So the real comparison is never "anonymity versus no anonymity" but "this risk versus that holder, under these restraints". [religion-risk](../religion/religion-risk.md) reached the same shape from a different direction: structure loads the gun, power pulls the trigger. The dangerous variable was not the text but who held it; here it is not the anonymity but who holds its exception.

What makes this more than a debating move is the asymmetry in what the key buys. Against the harms it is sold for it buys little: the Korean key produced no significant fall in illegal postings and pushed users offshore, and the UK key is conceded by its own regulator to be routable around with an app costing pennies. Against everyone else it buys a great deal, because the holder acquires a standing capability over the whole population, and that capability does not expire when the threat does. The one thing the key does buy, consistently, is the marginal case — the careless commenter, the adolescent who will not bother with a VPN — which is worth something, and is not what any of these mechanisms were sold as being for.

**The objection to E, which I think stands.** It relocates the question without answering it. Even a well-restrained key changes behaviour by existing: Penney's readers were not being prosecuted, they were being *watched*, and that alone is associated with a conservatively-estimated 10% fall in views of lawful articles. A key held under impeccable supervision still produces that effect, because the chilling runs on the belief that someone could look, not on anyone actually looking. So E cannot close the balance by pointing to good governance of the key. It can only insist that the balance be computed about a specific key in specific hands, which is a demand for precision rather than an answer.

## Where it stands

**The three legs, in order.** *Illegality* held, in a narrower form than I stated it: network-blind anonymity is the operating condition for particular markets — the investigating agencies say so themselves — but it raises the cost of enforcement rather than defeating it, and the displacement evidence says closing those markets moves the trade rather than shrinking the demand behind it. The leg survives as "anonymity makes this cheaper and more convenient", not as "anonymity is why this exists". *Disinformation* moved: the propagation of fake news sources sits with about one account in a thousand, which is not the mass of unaccountable accounts my reason assumed — though what I wanted to say instead, that named sources outperform anonymous ones, I could not establish and have withdrawn. *Bot farms* half-inverted. The verification market inverts it cleanly: identity is bought at a published price and rented at industrial scale, so identity checks are a line item. But the case I first reached for, the Internet Research Agency, turned out to cut the other way — its stolen identities were payment plumbing while the public operation ran on invented personas, which is first-rung anonymity and therefore my own original reason. I had that backwards and the correction belongs on the record.

**Where that leaves the question I set myself in advance.** I pre-registered three possible outcomes: (i) the position holds even after its weaker legs fall; (ii) it holds only for part of the spectrum, most likely for network-blind anonymity and not for pseudonymity, in which case the verdict is C or D rather than A; (iii) it falls and the contest is between B and E. The answer is **(ii)**. What I started with survives as a claim about one rung and a short list of harms, not as a claim about anonymity.

**But the verdict I did not expect is about the other half of the question.** Even granting the narrowed A in full, I cannot get from it to anything to do. Every available mechanism for having less anonymity has now been tried somewhere and measured: South Korea's identity verification produced no significant fall in illegal postings and pushed its users offshore, the UK's age assurance was routed around within hours by an app costing pennies and its own regulator concedes it cannot stop that, and the verification requirements already in place are priced into the fake-account business as an operating cost. What those mechanisms reliably do is tax the incidental user — the reader of an encyclopedia, the person whose legal name is a hazard — while the organised actor pays the toll and proceeds. The dial exists. It turns almost exclusively for the people I was not worried about.

**My best current view, then, is split, and the split is the result.** On the diagnosis I have moved less than I thought I would: anonymity at the third rung does carry real, concentrated harm, and in a free country the local balance is *worse* than the global one — the Tor harm estimates cluster in free countries, not unfree ones, which is the finding in this note I like least and believe most. On the remedy I have moved a long way, but not all the way to "nothing works". The mechanisms do something, and what they do is consistent across two independent bodies of evidence: Korea's identification requirement measurably reduced uninhibited posting while leaving illegal posting alone, and the verification market prices abuse rather than preventing it. Both say the same thing — these levers reach the careless and the marginal, and not the committed. So the lever is not fake; it is just aimed at a different population than the one the harms come from, and it collects its cost from everyone standing in between.

Which leaves E as the only question with any purchase. Not *should anonymity exist* — it does, and it will — but *who gets the exception, how narrow is it, and what happens to them when they abuse it*. That is a question about accountable power, and it is answerable in a way the original one is not.

**What would change my mind.** On the diagnosis: a defensible estimate showing the benefits of anonymity are large and countable after all, which would require solving the visibility problem rather than asserting past it. On the remedy: one jurisdiction where an identity requirement measurably reduced the harms it targeted without displacing users elsewhere and without falling hardest on the exposed — that is the finding Korea failed to produce, and if it exists somewhere I have not looked, D comes back and most of this section is wrong. On E: evidence that a lawful-access power, once granted, has ever been narrowed again without a scandal forcing it.

**How easily each of these falls**, since knowing which arguments to trust was the point of writing it down. Firmest: the verification-price evidence, which is published, recent and quantitative, and the Cho/Kim/Acquisti finding on Korean comments, which is peer-reviewed and measures what it claims. Solid: the concentration of fake-news propagation; the user-side Tor estimates and their free-country skew; the displacement finding on market seizures. Contested, and to be stated as such: the request-side Tor figures, which do not survive their own methodology; and the FBI's account of how the Silk Road server was found, which Ulbricht's expert and independent researchers dispute. Weakest, and not to be leaned on at all: the claim that disinformation travels better under real names, withdrawn for lack of a source; the two VPN disclosure cases, which are two incidents and not a pattern, and in one of which the records turned out not to be what identified the suspect; and the Internet Research Agency as evidence of anything about anonymity, since on inspection it runs the other way.

## Threads to pull

- **Does disinformation actually travel better under a real name?** I assumed it and could not source it. The question is researchable — compare reach per unit of exposure for named versus pseudonymous accounts on the same claim — and until someone has, the authority-of-the-name argument should not be used by either side.
- **Can the benefit of anonymity be measured at all?** The visibility asymmetry is the methodological core of the whole dispute. Survey and natural-experiment designs exist for chilled behaviour; whether anything can capture speech that happened *because* of anonymity is the harder half.
- **Has a lawful-access power ever been narrowed without a scandal?** E rests on supervision being real. The historical record of key-holder powers, once granted, is the test.
- **Freedom of speech and its limits** — the obvious sibling note for `society/`, and the one this note keeps brushing against without entering.
- **Surveillance as its own question** — this note treats being watched only where it bears on authorship; the broader case needs its own treatment.
- **Content moderation** — the third `society/` sibling: who decides what is removed, by what process, reviewable by whom.
- **Age assurance as a live natural experiment** — the UK data will mature. If the harms it targeted fall measurably and the circumvention plateaus, D comes back and this note needs revising *(as of 2026-10)*.

## Sources

Grouped by the argument each source does work for. Four things this section exists to record, all of them losses.

**The refutation pass.** A reviewer in a fresh context was given the finished note, the contract it had to satisfy, and the saved raw sources, and was briefed to *refute* it rather than check it. It returned twenty-five findings and I accepted almost all of them. Four changed the content rather than the wording: the Internet Research Agency case runs opposite to the use I made of it (see below); Penney's 30% is a headline figure he himself decomposes to about 10%; peer-reviewed work on the Korean real-name law contradicted my claim that it bought no civility; and a quotation criticising the Tor hidden-services study was attributed in my draft to that study's own authors when it is the Tor Project's objection against them — my own Sources entry had it right, so the note contradicted itself.

The most useful finding was not factual but logical: my refutation of conjecture C attacked something C does not claim. C is about network-blind anonymity, and I had argued against it using UK age-assurance rules, which operate at the first two rungs and were routed around within hours — an outcome that *confirms* C. Block 6 now says so. The pass also caught a self-serving asymmetry the contract forbids: every other conjecture had a dedicated refutation while the surviving core of my own entry position had none, and was shielded by a sentence ("nothing I read moved it") that no observation could contradict. That block now carries two objections and the sentence is gone. Smaller corrections — unit slips between exposures and shares, users and traffic, hourly and daily percentages; a cherry-picked price range; an unsourced price; a dangling reference to outcomes "set out in advance" that appeared only in the working spec; missing `(as of)` markers — are folded into the entries below.

**What I did not accept.** One finding argued that Grinberg's panel should not be described as "users" at all, since it is registered voters matched to Twitter accounts. I kept the word: the paper's own abstract speaks of users, and the note states the matching explicitly. Everything else material was adopted.

**What the sourcing refused me.** I set out to write that the most effective disinformation travels under real names, because a name lends borrowed authority. Neither paper I had in mind supports it: Grinberg et al. measure concentration of sharing and exposure, Mosleh and Rand measure an elite channel without comparing it to an anonymous one, and neither discusses anonymity. The claim is withdrawn from the argument and demoted to a thread to pull. Separately, the 80%-of-Tor-requests figure I had half-remembered as settled turned out to rest on a crawl with an unknown denominator, and the correction came from the Tor Project rather than from any critic of anonymity.

**What went against the entry position.** Two things. The displacement research on darknet market seizures says closures move the trade rather than shrinking the demand behind it, which converts the illegality leg from "anonymity is why this harm exists" into "anonymity makes it cheaper" — a real demotion of the strongest reason I had. And Penney's chilling-effect data shows anonymity doing work for people committing no offence at all, which is the part of B that no argument about whistleblowers can reach.

**What went against the case I was building against the entry position**, which is the more uncomfortable list. Jardine, Lindner and Owenson find the harms of Tor concentrated in free countries rather than unfree ones, so the defensive value I was leaning on is weakest exactly where this argument is being conducted. Cho, Kim and Acquisti find that Korea's real-name requirement did measurably reduce uninhibited posting, which contradicts the flat "it delivered nothing" I had drafted. And the Internet Research Agency, which I had used as the showpiece of identity-theft-not-anonymity, turns out on reading the indictment to have run its public operation on invented personas — first-rung anonymity, and therefore evidence for the reason I was trying to overturn.

**Left unverified.** The Cambridge verification-price index is cited from the university's own write-up, dated 11 December 2025, which states that the analysis appears in *Science* without giving the article's own date; I did not obtain the article, so the figures are as that write-up gives them. Owen and Savage's article is paywalled and I have read only its abstract and others' accounts of it. The two VPN disclosure cases are from secondary accounts rather than the filings. A price I had quoted for aged, ID-verified accounts sold in bulk has been removed from the note: I could not source it to anything citable.

*Concentration and channels of disinformation*
- N. Grinberg, K. Joseph, L. Friedland, B. Swire-Thompson, D. Lazer, "Fake news on Twitter during the 2016 U.S. presidential election," *Science* 363 (2019), doi:10.1126/science.aau2706 — the concentration figures quoted verbatim (1% of individuals for 80% of exposures, 0.1% for nearly 80% of shares) and the finding that mainstream outlets remained most people's main political exposure. Does **not** speak to anonymity.
- M. Mosleh, D. G. Establishes that a large identified channel exists; does **not** compare it to an anonymous one.

*The bot-farm inversion*
- J. Roozenbeek, A. Dek, S. van der Linden (Cambridge Social Decision-Making Lab), verification-price index across 500+ platforms, July 2024–July 2025, via <https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/price-bot-army-global-index> — per-verification prices ($0.08 Russia, $0.10 UK, $0.26 US, $4.93 Japan, $3.24 Australia) and the authors' reading that higher SIM costs are "likely to suppress rates of malicious online activity", which is the basis for calling identity rules a tax rather than a wall.
- Europol, Operation SIMCARTEL — a cybercrime-as-a-service network renting access to SIM farms, through which more than 49 million fake online accounts are estimated to have been created, using SIM cards bought from almost 80 countries. (My first draft had this as accounts "across more than 80 countries", which reverses what the figure counts, and described the accounts as bought wholesale rather than created through a rented service.)
- United States v. Internet Research Agency et al. (indictment, 16 February 2018), <https://www.justice.gov/d9/fieldable-panel-panes/basic-panes/attachments/2018/02/16/internet_research_agency_indictment.pdf> — four bank accounts and five PayPal accounts opened with the stolen identities of real US persons, and aggravated identity theft charged against four defendants. **Cited against my own first draft:** I had used this as the case that bot operations run on stolen identity rather than anonymity, and had miscounted the PayPal accounts as six. On reading the document, the stolen identities are the payment and advertising infrastructure, while the public-facing groups — "Blacktivist", "Secured Borders", "United Muslims of America", "Heart of Texas" — were invented personas, with further accounts opened "in the names of false and fictitious U.S. personas". *Enforcement without a key*

*The harm that does not depend on the channel*
- Europol, Internet Organised Crime Threat Assessment — Tor and its hidden services "facilitate practically untraceable sexual exploitation of children by allowing the exchange of images anonymously"; anonymisation chaining through successive VPN and Tor nodes as a technique that complicates attribution; more than 120 active ransomware brands observed in 2025 *(as of 2026-10)*. This is the source for the strong form of the illegality leg, and it is the investigating agency's own characterisation.
- Australian Institute of Criminology, research on the impact of darknet market seizures on opioid availability — rapid vendor and buyer migration between marketplaces, dispersal and substitution after closures, seizures reducing opioid and fentanyl availability and raising prices only once several major markets had fallen, and the authors' conclusion that closures are unlikely to change public consumption because new markets emerge to meet existing demand. **Cited against the illegality leg as I first stated it:** it is what forced "anonymity is why this exists" down to "anonymity makes this cheaper".

*The middle rung, tried*
- D. Cho, S. Kim, A.
