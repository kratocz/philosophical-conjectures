# What can one person do against misinformation in a public thread — and for whom?

*Status: open · last touched 2026-10-20 · sources checked 2026-10-20*

## The question

Someone posts confident, error-ridden claims to an audience. You can answer, mock, ignore, or report. Which of these does any good, and for whom? The question is narrower than "how should society fight misinformation". It is about one person, one thread, and limited evenings.

It splits into four targets that pull in different directions:

- **the author** of the claims, who is usually the least movable person in the thread;
- **the audience**: people who read without commenting, some of them undecided;
- **the information environment**: every reply is also engagement, and engagement is distribution;
- **yourself**: time, temper, and the risk of becoming the thing you are answering.

This note began from a long public exchange, archived elsewhere and kept out of this repository. When I asked how to defend against people who spread such claims, seven working rules came out of it:

1. inoculate in advance;
2. don't amplify;
3. one checkable point instead of ten;
4. calm as a visible norm;
5. relationships over refutation;
6. support professional fact-checkers;
7. handle threats through channels, not arguments.

They were written from memory of the research. This note puts them against the sources, and the sourcing broke several of them. That is the main result.

I came in with two intuitions, written down before the research so that I could tell afterwards whether they survived:

1. **"Nobody with an IQ over 100 can just believe that, surely? When it has so many logical errors…"** I said it, in Czech, after reading an inventory of the logical errors in a single post; this is a translation.
2. **Let him write, and mockery will make him expose himself faster.** This was my earlier strategy in that exchange, and it came with a worry: it would be worse if he started mixing true facts into the falsehoods. Unlike the first intuition, this one was not fresh. By the time it was written down, I had already watched it fail in the exchange, so its verdict below is less of a test.

## Conjectures

- **A — Intelligence protects.** The errors are visible to anyone who looks. The danger lies with less able readers, and a smart audience is safe. *(Entry intuition 1.)*
- **B — Let it burn.** Confident nonsense discredits itself, and ridicule speeds that up. *(Entry intuition 2.)*
- **C — Correct the record.** State what is false, why, and what is true instead. Corrections work.
- **D — Inoculate first.** Teach the techniques before people meet them. This prevents more than any correction can repair, and it scales.
- **E — The audience, not the author.** The author will not be argued out of it. A reply is for the bystanders, so it should make one checkable point, stay calm, and pin dated claims to a criterion agreed in advance.
- **F — Relationships over refutation.** People leave such beliefs through relationships with people who did not humiliate them, not by being refuted.

## Refutations & tensions

### Against A: half right, and wrong where it matters most

The literature splits on A, and the split is the finding.

**For A.** Pennycook and Rand (2019) used the Cognitive Reflection Test, which measures the disposition to stop and think rather than raw intelligence. Scores were "positively correlated with the ability to discern fake news from real news - even for headlines that align with individuals' political ideology". Their title says it: *lazy, not biased*. On this account, people fall for implausible headlines mostly because they do not stop to think, and those who do stop do better. That is the strongest case for A, and it is a real one.

**Against A.** Stanovich, West and Toplak summarise a research programme on *myside bias*, the tendency to evaluate evidence and generate arguments in favour of a view one already holds. They call the result "somewhat surprising": "The magnitude of the myside bias shows very little relation to intelligence." In their framing, avoiding myside bias is a thinking skill that intelligence tests do not measure. An earlier paper is narrower still. It found "very little evidence that participants of higher cognitive ability displayed less natural myside bias". Here *natural* means bias shown when people get no instruction or cue to avoid it, and the participants were university students.

**The two findings fit together.** Spotting an implausible claim is a reflection task, and reflection helps. Weighing a claim about one's own side is a myside task, and ability gives little protection there. The reader who matters in a thread is often the second kind: someone already partly on the author's side. For that reader, Tappin, Pennycook and Rand (2021) add a further twist. Cognitive sophistication did not magnify the effect of political identity on judgement, but it did magnify the effect of prior factual beliefs. The famous stronger claim was Kahan and colleagues' "motivated numeracy": that polarisation "did not abate among subjects highest in numeracy; instead, it increased". It failed a large preregistered replication. Persson et al. (2021) wrote: "We did not find good evidence for motivated numeracy". I had cited Kahan from memory as settled. It isn't.

**The premise "this many errors" is also suspect.** In the exchange that prompted this note, the errors became visible only after hours of work: fetching primary sources raw, checking quotations by script, and setting sentences from different paragraphs side by side. A reader scrolling past does none of that. That is one case, not evidence, but it is a reason to doubt that "this many errors" describes what a reader actually experiences.

**A, as I stated it, fails.** "Nobody over 100 can believe this" is false. What survives is narrower: reflection protects against claims that look implausible, and offers little protection against claims that flatter one's side. The audience is divided less by ability than by allegiance.

### Against B: ridicule is repetition with a weak tag

The Debunking Handbook 2020 states the mechanism plainly. Repeat the myth "only once, directly prior to the correction"; beyond that, "needless repetitions of the misinformation should be avoided: Although backfire effects are uncommon, we know that repetition makes information appear true". Fazio and colleagues found that "illusory truth effects occurred even when participants knew better". Their wording is careful: participants "sometimes" rely on fluency even when they have the knowledge. Fazio's statements were repeated with no correction attached. Ridicule sits between that and a proper correction. It repeats the claim with a sneer as the only signal that it is false, and it rarely adds what is true instead. B's mechanism, "let it run and mock it", maximises exactly the repetition the Handbook warns against.

The exchange adds an illustration, not evidence. The author's text had ridicule built in as a credential: rejection counted as a sign of his calling, and mockers were declared irrelevant in advance. In that kind of frame, a sneer arrives pre-interpreted as persecution. What I observed was his escalation each time I opened with mockery, and one civil reply after I de-escalated. That is one author, not the audience B is about.

The worry attached to B, that mixing true facts in would be worse, came true anyway: later he brought facts that were true, with a conclusion that did not follow from them.

**B fails** on the repetition mechanism. On its effect on bystanders, I found no direct study either way *(as of 2026-10)*.

### Against C: corrections work, and the details were folklore

C comes out better than I expected, and two pieces of advice I had given came out worse.

The fear that correcting a myth entrenches it, the *backfire effect*, is mostly unfounded. Wood and Porter tested 52 issues with more than 10,100 subjects and "found no corrections capable of triggering backfire". Ecker, Lewandowsky and Chadwick found that even for audiences meeting the myth for the first time, "it is safe to repeat misinformation when correcting it". Together with the Handbook's "only once", that cuts my rule *don't amplify* down. Repeating the myth once, inside a correction, is fine. What survives is a hypothesis about the platform: a reply is engagement, and engagement may lift a post in other people's feeds. I have not sourced it.

Corrections also work when ordinary users make them. In Bode and Vraga's simulated news feed about the Zika virus, corrections posted by other users were as effective as algorithmic ones, and "correction occurs for both high and low conspiracy belief individuals". That is the closest any source here comes to the situation this note is about: a bystander correcting a post in front of an audience.

The Handbook also says how corrections should look: "it is important to provide detailed refutations" that explain why the claim is false and what is true instead. That sits uneasily with my rule *one checkable point instead of ten*; see E.

The *truth sandwich* is a template the Handbook recommends: fact, then a warning about the myth, then the fallacy explained, then the fact again. I had attributed the name to Lakoff. In the CNN interview of June 2018 where it was coined, Lakoff described the method, but the host named it: "It's a truth sandwich." The Handbook never uses the phrase. More importantly, Swire-Thompson, Butler and Rapp (2025) tested the format directly. Both formats they compared "were highly effective", and "there was no evidence that the truth sandwich format enhanced the effectiveness". Their conclusion: "clear and detailed corrections can be powerfully effective against misinformation regardless of format".

What limits C is not backfire but persistence and scope. "Misinformation often continues to influence inferential reasoning after clear and credible corrections are provided", as Ecker and colleagues summarise the continued-influence effect. A correction reaches only those who read it, and it does nothing about the next claim. That is the opening for D.

### Against D: the strongest experimental programme, with one live dispute

The founding study is Roozenbeek and van der Linden's 2019 evaluation of *Bad News*, a game in which players produce fake news. Players' ability to spot misinformation improved "irrespective of education, age, political ideology, and cognitive style". The paper admits "We thus lack a traditional control group". The sample was self-selected, and the design compared the same players before and after. The authors anticipated the obvious objection, that players might simply distrust everything. They included real-news control items and report that "participants did not meaningfully adjust their ratings of the 'real' control items". They add that these items may have confounded reliability with familiarity.

The 2022 follow-up is much stronger. It ran six randomised trials of short prebunking videos, with *discernment* as the primary outcome: the difference between ratings of manipulative and neutral content. The videos "increase people's ability to discern trustworthy from untrustworthy content". The YouTube field study measured only recognition of the techniques. It found significant effects for both videos. Only one of them reached the preregistered threshold; the other "narrowly failed" it.

The dispute concerns the games, not the videos. Modirrousta-Galian and Higham (2023) reanalysed five studies with a method that separates discernment from response bias. They found that "when comparable true and fake news items were used, Bad News and Go Viral! did not improve discrimination, but rather elicited more 'false' responses to all news items". On that reading, the games make players more sceptical of true news too, not better at telling true from false. That cuts against the 2019 control-item result, and I have not checked whether the original authors replied.

D keeps the advantage I gave it: it works before the claim arrives and it transfers across topics. In the exchange, the one pattern worth teaching was a specific one: *a challenged prediction gets relabelled as a mere estimate after the fact*. It recurs wherever dated predictions are made. Whether teaching patterns builds discernment or only distrust has a partial answer: the videos improved discernment in randomised trials, while the games are disputed. For one person in a thread, though, D is hard to deliver. It belongs before the thread, not in it.

### Against E: a synthesis without its own evidence

E is the position I came to favour in the exchange itself, so it needs the hardest look, and it has the least direct evidence of the six.

**Its premise about the author is undercut.** Costello, Pennycook and Rand (2024) gave conspiracy believers a short, evidence-based dialogue with a language model, tailored to each person's stated reasons. "The intervention reduced conspiracy belief by ~20%", and the effect lasted two months. Believers, then, are not immune to argument. Two caveats apply:

- **The paper is under an editorial cloud.** *Science* attached an Editorial Expression of Concern on 11 June 2026. It cites dataset problems that "made it challenging to reproduce some of the specific values" and inconsistent screening criteria. According to Retraction Watch, the authors' reanalysis produces results that "match those in the original article in direction, statistical significance, and substantive size", "which the journal is now evaluating" *(as of 2026-06)*.
- **The setting differs.** The dialogue was private, patient, and came from a source with no social stake in the believer losing face. Whether that works in public, from a known opponent and in front of the believer's own audience, is untested as far as I found.

**Its advice to the audience is half supported.** Bode and Vraga support the core of it: users correcting in public do move readers, including conspiracy-minded ones. But the specific form E recommends is not supported:

- **"One checkable point" contradicts the sources.** The Handbook and Swire-Thompson both favour *detailed* refutations. My reason for one point was about debate dynamics, not about correction: a long list lets the author pick its weakest item and ignore the rest. That may be true of a hostile author, and it is an anecdote. Against the evidence on readers, it is a conjecture at best.
- **"Calm as a visible norm" is untested here.** No source in this note measures it.
- **The criterion-in-advance move rests on an assumption.** It assumes that if the author refuses a criterion, readers will notice. An audience that already reads rejection as persecution may read the demand for a criterion the same way. That is the same objection I raised against B.

**E survives only as a hypothesis.** Its sound part is the focus on the audience, which is better supported by C's evidence than by anything specific to E. Its distinctive advice has no direct evidence. The test that could refute it would be to compare one-point and detailed replies in a public thread, measuring how undecided readers change. I found no such study *(as of 2026-10)*.

### Against F: exits are social, but not mainly through friendships

F claims the most and has the least comparative evidence. Altier, Leonard Boyle, Shortland and Horgan coded 87 autobiographies of people who left terrorist groups. They found that "push, rather than pull, factors are more commonly cited". The pushes are disillusionment with the group's strategy and actions, "disagreements with group leaders or members", and burnout. Those count for more than abandoning the ideology. Some of that is interpersonal, but it concerns relationships *inside* the group, not new ones outside it. New outside relationships are a pull factor, and pull factors were cited less. Applying exits from terrorism to people who spread online misinformation is a leap, and I flag it as one.

Engel, Phadke and Mitra (2023) interviewed former conspiracy believers, and their findings cut both ways. Disillusionment came through "exposure to inconsistencies in theories or toxicity". That is partly argument, met at the right moment. Recovery afterwards was helped by "empathy and solidarity in fellow former believers". Those bridges were to other leavers, not to former opponents. Both studies are qualitative or autobiographical, and neither compares relationships with arguments directly. I found none that does *(as of 2026-10)*.

So F weakens to this: people leave mostly because of their experience inside the group, and arguments land when the person is ready for them. The relationships that help afterwards are mostly with others who have left. "Don't humiliate" survives as plain decency and as a guess about the future, not as a finding.

## Where it stands

There is no single winner, and the ranking I brought into this note does not survive. My best current view, held provisionally:

- **Both entry intuitions failed as stated.** "Nobody over 100 can believe this" is false. Reflection helps against implausible claims, but ability does little against claims that flatter one's side, and the reader in a thread is often on the author's side. "Let it burn" fails on the repetition mechanism, and its effect on bystanders is unmeasured.
- **C has the best evidence for a single reply.** Corrections work, including corrections by ordinary users in front of an audience. Backfire is rare, and one repetition of the myth inside the correction is safe. The sources favour detailed corrections. Format matters less than clarity.
- **D has the strongest experimental programme**, but it is a tool for before the thread, not in it. The videos improved discernment in randomised trials. Whether the games teach discernment or only distrust is disputed.
- **E is a hypothesis.** Its focus on the audience is sound and borrows its support from C. Its specific advice is unsourced, and "one checkable point" runs against the sources. Its premise that the author is unmovable is undercut by private-dialogue evidence that is itself under an expression of concern. Whether public argument moves an author is untested.
- **F weakens** to "people leave mostly through their own experience inside the group". Relationships matter more for recovery than for leaving.

The seven rules, one by one:

1. **Inoculate in advance:** survives, with the games-versus-videos caveat.
2. **Don't amplify:** survives only as an unsourced hypothesis about feeds, not as a rule about repeating the myth.
3. **One checkable point instead of ten:** contradicted by the sources on readers. It survives only as a conjecture about hostile authors.
4. **Calm as a visible norm:** untested.
5. **Relationships over refutation:** withdrawn in that form.
6. **Support professional fact-checkers:** not tested here. This note did not examine fact-checking organisations.
7. **Handle threats through platform and legal channels:** never needed research.

**What would change my mind:**

- If a study compared one-point and detailed public replies and found that one point moves undecided readers more, rule 3 and E would recover.
- If the original authors answered Modirrousta-Galian and Higham with game data that shows improved discrimination, D's dispute would close in its favour.
- If Costello et al. are corrected or retracted, the "author is movable" line weakens. If the result replicates in a public setting, E's premise is wrong outright.
- If evidence showed that public ridicule shifts undecided bystanders more than it hardens believers, B would come back as a tactic aimed at the audience.
- If there were measurements of how much a critical reply lifts a post's reach, rule 2 would become a rule or be dropped.

## Threads to pull

- **Discernment vs. scepticism in the games.** Is there a reply from Roozenbeek and colleagues to Modirrousta-Galian and Higham?
- **Does replying amplify?** Platform-ranking evidence on whether critical replies raise a post's distribution.
- **Public vs. private persuasion.** Has anyone tested an argument's effect on a believer in front of their own audience?
- **The relabelling pattern** ("that was only an estimate") as a general mark of an unfalsifiable claim. It belongs with the dial argument in `religion/science-as-religion.md` and may deserve its own note.

**What the sourcing cost.** Four claims I had made from memory in the original conversation did not survive in the form I gave them:

- that intelligence makes bias worse (contested, not established);
- that the truth sandwich was Lakoff's term and the best format (neither);
- that "don't repeat the myth" belongs in a rule against amplifying (one repetition inside a correction is safe);
- that inoculation is simply proven (strong for videos, disputed for games).

"Relationships over refutation" lost its comparative claim. The first draft of this note added a fifth error of its own: a sentence saying that ability helped once participants were told to set their view aside. That sentence was from memory and is not in any source read. It has been removed.

**Refutation pass, 2026-10-20.** A fresh-context reviewer was briefed to refute the first draft and given the raw sources. I re-verified each quotation it relied on against the raw text before applying anything. It broke a good deal:

- **D undersold.** The draft cited the 2022 field study's near-miss and left out that the primary outcome of the RCTs was discernment, and that the outcome improved. It also left out the 2019 real-news control items. Modirrousta-Galian and Higham's critique concerns the games only, under the condition of comparable items. Both points were restored.
- **E held to a lower standard.** It had been held to a lower standard than A and B, and the verdict picked it with no source for its advice. The verdict was rebuilt without a winner. "One checkable point" is now marked as running against the sources, and Bode & Vraga was added.
- **A overstated.** "A fails" overstated the case and omitted Pennycook & Rand 2019. The sentence from memory was removed, and the section was rewritten as a split.
- **Identifying details.** The draft carried details that could identify the other party in the exchange; they were cut to the mechanism.
- **Anecdote ranked as evidence.** The exchange had been ranked above the literature in B, as evidence about the audience when it only showed the author. It is now an illustration.
- **Contradiction on repetition.** B and C were inconsistent about repetition. Both now use the Handbook's "only once" and "needless repetitions".
- **Rule tally.** The tally of the seven rules had silently reworded one rule and omitted another. It is now listed rule by rule.
- **Smaller fixes.** An inference from rating news items to doubting corrections was removed. F now records the in-group interpersonal factor, the fact that the bridge in Engel et al. runs to fellow leavers, and the leap from terrorism to misinformation. The verdict no longer states as found what Threads calls untested. The perishable dates were corrected.

**Not adopted:** the suggestion to move D above E. With E demoted to a hypothesis and D placed before the thread rather than in it, a single ranking no longer describes the result.
