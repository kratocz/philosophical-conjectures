# Who could end the war in Ukraine tomorrow — and what justifies not doing it?

*Status: open · last touched 2026-07-24 · sources checked 2026-07-24*

## The question

Two facts about the war in Ukraine are checkable in a way most claims about wars are
not, and I want this note to stand on them rather than on anyone's narrative — mine
included.

The first: Russian soldiers crossed into Ukraine, not Ukrainian soldiers into Russia.
Whatever story is told about threats, spheres of influence, and provocations, the
direction in which the physical attack ran is not a matter of interpretation. Call
this **the entry asymmetry**.

The second: the war could end tomorrow, and one party alone holds that option. If
Russia stops fighting and withdraws, the war is over. If Ukraine stops fighting,
Ukraine is over — the country, not the war. Call this **the exit asymmetry**.

Most public argument runs the other way around: it asks whether Ukraine's resistance
is still justified, or whether Western support for it is. Both questions are live, and
this note will reach them. But the asymmetries say the burden of justification sits
elsewhere first. A war that one side could end unilaterally, any morning, at the price
of going home, is being *prolonged* — daily, by choice. So the question I most want
answered is: **is Putin's refusal to end the war grounded in anything that survives
scrutiny?** Asking the attacked to justify resisting before asking the attacker to
justify continuing gets the order of justification backwards, and half the work of
this note is refusing that inversion.

One methodological move does much of the work, so I state it up front: I hold states
to the standard I already hold people to. I cannot beat a man in the street and be
acquitted because I felt he meant me harm; suspicion, however sincere, does not
license a physical attack. And the licence universalizes catastrophically: a world in
which every state may strike whenever it believes itself threatened is not a world
with fewer wars but a world of little else. I learned only later that this move has a
name and a literature — Walzer's "domestic analogy" [VERIFY], and in law the Caroline
standard for anticipatory self-defence beside Article 51's "if an armed attack
occurs" [VERIFY] — but I arrived at it the way most people do: by refusing to believe
that what is criminal for a person becomes statesmanship when done with an army. The
literature is a support here, not an authority.

Three seams I will keep visible, because arguments about this war usually blur them:

1. **Legal versus moral.** The UN Charter's verdict and a moral verdict are different
   instruments. I believe they agree here; I want to know it from checking, not from
   wanting it.
2. **Starting versus continuing versus conduct.** The justice of the 2014 seizure and
   the 2022 invasion, the justice of continuing the war in 2026, and the justice of
   how either side fights (jus in bello) are three questions, not one. This note takes
   a position on the first two and deliberately not the third — pointers only.
3. **Fact versus standard.** The asymmetries are facts. That they are *decisive* —
   that entry fixes the aggressor and exit fixes the burden — is a standard, and
   standards have to earn their keep. The refutations section is where mine take
   their beating.

What follows from the answer is the rest of the original question. If the
prolongation is unjustified, two further verdicts come under pressure: whether
Ukraine's defence has a limit it is obliged to respect — must the attacked ever
settle? — and what third parties, my own country among them, owe: is support
permitted, obligatory, sufficient? Those are the three layers the public debate runs
together. Here they arrive as consequences of the asymmetries, in that order.

**The war as of 2026-07.** The dated facts this note stands on, gathered in one place
so their aging is visible; the argument is built to survive their drift, but this is
where it touches the ground. Russia's full-scale invasion began on 24 February 2022;
the war itself began in 2014 with the seizure of Crimea and the outbreak of war in
the Donbas, and both continue (as of 2026-07). Short truces in April and May 2026 did
not hold; US-brokered negotiations stalled in March 2026 and are being revived this
month (as of 2026-07). The front moves slowly: about 31 square kilometres of
Ukrainian territory changed hands in Russia's favour over the thirty days to 30 June
2026 (The Economist's estimate). Military casualties — killed, wounded, missing — are
estimated at roughly 1.2–1.4 million on the Russian side and 500–600 thousand on the
Ukrainian, approaching two million combined (CSIS estimates, as of 2026-06); the UN
human rights office has verified 16,431 civilians killed while stressing that the
real toll is higher (OHCHR, as of 2026-06). About 5.9 million Ukrainians remain
refugees abroad and 3.7 million are displaced within the country (UNHCR, as of
2025-12). Western support since 2022 runs to hundreds of billions of euros in total;
Europe's military aid alone totals about €69 billion (Kiel Institute tracker, as of
2026-04). Ukraine continues to fight, and what an end would require — of Russia, and
of Ukraine — is again being negotiated this month (as of 2026-07).

Why me, and why now? I am Czech. My country was priced out of its borders by third
parties in 1938, in a settlement celebrated abroad as peace; "about us, without us"
is not an abstraction here but a national memory — and I will have to be careful,
later in this note, about what that memory does and does not license me to infer. And
"now" because July 2026 is one of the months in which this note's question is being
answered in practice — by negotiators, by parliaments voting on aid, by every day the
war continues — mostly without anyone stating the standard they are using. This note
is me trying to state mine.

## Conjectures

Five verdicts compete. Each is stated at full strength — the hedging is the next
section's job. A, B and C would each dissolve my question or answer it against me; D
concedes most of my premises and still ends somewhere else; E is mine.

- **Conjecture A — The Russian case for war.** The prolongation is justified because
  the war itself is: NATO's eastward expansion was an existential threat gathering at
  Russia's border — a promise of membership stood on the record since Bucharest 2008
  [VERIFY] — and no nuclear power waits for the noose to close; the Russian-speaking
  population of the Donbas needed protection from a state that had been shelling it
  since 2014; the West itself wrote the permission slip for redrawing borders in
  Kosovo [VERIFY]; and the recognized republics of Donetsk and Luhansk invited the
  intervention [VERIFY]. As for 2026: the annexed oblasts are now constitutionally
  Russian territory [VERIFY], Western security guarantees have proven worthless to
  those who trusted them, and a ceasefire that leaves the "root causes" unresolved is
  not peace but an armistice before a worse war. On this view, continuing is the
  responsible course: only a settlement that fixes the causes — neutrality,
  demilitarization, recognition — ends the war rather than postponing it.

- **Conjecture B — Nobody's war is just.** War as a method is beyond justification —
  the deliberate organization of killing cannot be redeemed by the justice of anyone's
  cause. Every day of this war is a moral failure of all parties: Russia's for waging
  it, Ukraine's for feeding its people into it, the West's for fueling it with weapons
  and calling that solidarity. A proxy contest between blocs is being fought with
  Ukrainian bodies. The only demand that survives moral scrutiny is: stop shooting —
  everyone, now, unconditionally. Whoever keeps arguing about who may shoot whom has
  already changed the subject from the only thing that matters.

- **Conjecture C — The realist dissolution.** The question of this note is malformed.
  There is no world government; above states stands nothing that could give
  "justified" a purchase. Great powers respond to shifts in the balance of power as
  weather systems respond to pressure gradients — moralizing about it obscures rather
  than explains. The West expanded its sphere into what Russia had declared vital;
  Russia reacted the way great powers predictably react [VERIFY: Mearsheimer];
  Ukraine's tragedy is geography. The real questions are prudential ones — what
  escalates, what deters, what a stable equilibrium looks like — and dressing them in
  the vocabulary of justice is how wars get longer, not shorter.

- **Conjecture D — A just defense meets its limit.** The invasion was unjust and the
  defense was just — and justification is not a title held once but a balance run
  continually. Proportionality binds the defender too (jus ex bello [VERIFY:
  Moellendorf]): at some point the goods still recoverable by fighting no longer
  outweigh what the fighting costs — the dead, the maimed, a generation abroad, a
  demography broken, a nuclear tail-risk that no territorial claim can price. There
  was, on this view, at least one moment when a better peace was available — Istanbul,
  spring 2022 [VERIFY] — and every month since has bought less at higher cost. Past
  that point the duty flips: the defender's duty is to negotiate seriously, and the
  third parties' duty flips from arming the defense to pressing both sides toward the
  exit. Support that merely sustains a lost equilibrium is not solidarity; it is
  cruelty with good intentions.

- **Conjecture E — Two asymmetries and a burden.** Mine. Who the aggressor is, is not
  a judgment but a fact: Russian soldiers crossed the border — the entry asymmetry —
  and suspicion licenses nothing, for states as for men in the street. Who keeps the
  war alive is equally a fact: Russia could end it any morning by going home; Ukraine
  could only end its own existence — the exit asymmetry. Together they fix where the
  burden of justification lies, and nothing in A survives scrutiny to meet it — so
  the prolongation is unjust twice over, legally and morally. From the same two
  facts, the defense needs no external permission and answers to no outside
  proportionality committee: the price of freedom is weighed by those paying it — the
  standing argument. And support is not charity but obligation, under-delivered: the
  states that took Ukraine's nuclear weapons in exchange for assurances signed more
  than sentiment — Budapest, 1994 [VERIFY] — and my country knows from 1938 what it
  costs when third parties price another nation's borders for it.
