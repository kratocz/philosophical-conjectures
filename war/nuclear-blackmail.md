# Can a nuclear threat change what is right?

*Status: open · last touched 2026-07-27 · sources checked 2026-07-27*

## The question

The previous note in this folder owes a debt, and this note exists to collect it.
[[ukraine-war-justification]] reached its verdict on Western support while leaning,
twice, on a sentence it never argued for. In the refutations:

> On the nuclear tail: E absorbs it as a constraint on *how* — which capabilities,
> which targets, what pace — never on *whether*; because if nuclear threats can
> defeat a defense outright, nuclear blackmail becomes a master key that opens any
> neighbor, and the lesson every state on a nuclear border learns is: get warheads.

And in the verdict itself:

> And the nuclear tail stays absorbed as a constraint on *how*, never on
> *whether*: the alternative is a world in which warheads are the only sovereignty
> worth having — the proliferation lesson Budapest already teaches once.

Call this **the audited sentence** — the IOU. It sounded firm when I wrote it. It
was also unearned: an assertion doing a verdict's work with no argument of its own
underneath. This note is the audit, and an audit of one's own promissory note has
to say in advance what counts as failure. So, before any argument, the possible
findings, registered now so I cannot move the goalposts later:

- **(i) Paid.** The line between managing nuclear risk ("how") and yielding to
  nuclear coercion ("whether") can be drawn and defended. The Ukraine note stands.
- **(ii) Paid with amendment.** A line exists, but not the one I wrote; the
  sentence overpromised and must be reformulated. The Ukraine note gets corrected,
  visibly, and the correction recorded in both notes.
- **(iii) Defaulted.** No defensible line exists. The Ukraine note's verdict on
  support loses a premise and must be reweighed — also visibly, also in both.

Whatever falls out, it gets written into both notes. That is the deal this
repository is built on: the conjecture may be bolder than the verdict, but the
books have to balance.

The question behind the debt is larger than the war that raised it: **can a
nuclear threat change what is right?** Not whether it changes what is *prudent* —
of course it does; any sane actor behaves differently when annihilation enters the
room. The question is whether it changes *permissions and obligations*: whether a
defense that was justified stops being justified, whether support that was
obligatory stops being obligatory, the moment the aggressor's threats go nuclear.
Three seams to keep visible:

1. **Prudence versus permission.** "It would be wise to slow down" and "you are no
   longer entitled to proceed" are different sentences; most arguments about
   escalation blur them within a paragraph.
2. **Deterrence versus compellence.** A threat made to *preserve* a status quo and
   a threat made to *change* one — to take, to hold, to force surrender — may
   carry different moral weight; the distinction has a literature [VERIFY:
   Schelling], and the word "blackmail" in this note's title assumes it can be
   drawn. That assumption is itself on trial here.
3. **The defender versus the bystanders.** What Ukraine may do under nuclear
   threat and what its supporters owe under the same threat are separate
   questions; the audited sentence was about the second.

**The nuclear record as of 2026-07.** The dated facts, in one place; the argument
must survive their aging, and this is where it touches ground. Russia has run
nuclear signaling as a policy instrument throughout the full-scale war: forces on
"special combat readiness" three days in (2022-02); the threat peak of autumn
2022, when, by published accounts, Washington privately warned that nuclear use
would be answered with conventional destruction of Russia's forces, and no weapon
was used; a doctrine signed on 19 November 2024 lowering the declared threshold —
attack by a non-nuclear state with a nuclear power's "participation or support"
counts as a joint attack — with analysts split on whether the paper changed
anything real; tactical weapons deployed to Belarus (2023); the Oreshnik
intermediate-range strike on Dnipro (21 November 2024), billed as retaliation for
Western-missile strikes into Russia, with systems later stationed in Belarus (as
of 2026-01). Against the signaling, the record of follow-through: HIMARS (2022),
Western tanks (2023-01), Storm Shadow (2023-05), ATACMS (2023-10), F-16s (2024),
Ukrainian troops on Russian soil in Kursk (2024-08), Western-missile strikes
inside Russia (2024-11) — each crossed a declared or implied red line; none was
answered with a nuclear weapon (as of 2026-07). A US intelligence assessment from
2025 judged nuclear use "very unlikely" absent an existential threat to the
regime. The scholarly base rate points the same way: in the fullest dataset of
militarized compellent threats (1918–2001), nuclear-armed coercers succeeded no
more often than non-nuclear ones — nuclear weapons deter superbly and compel
badly (Sechser and Fuhrmann's dataset of 200-plus cases). Meanwhile the wider
architecture thinned: New START expired on 5 February 2026 with no successor —
the first time in five decades without binding limits on the two largest
arsenals — Moscow declaring a conditional moratorium on exceeding the old
ceilings (as of 2026-02). And the disarmament ledger this note keeps returning
to: Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan gave up inherited arsenals for assurances
(1994); South Africa built six weapons and verifiably dismantled them (announced
1993) — the only state ever to roll back a completed arsenal of its own.

One admission belongs up front, because it does not go away: I live under NATO's
nuclear umbrella. My country's safety — my safety — is purchased daily by exactly
the conditional intentions this note is about to examine. If the audit ends in a
place I cannot inhabit — if it concludes that the umbrella's threats are
impermissible, or that yielding is obligatory — consistency will present its bill
to me, not to an abstraction. The refutations return to this under its own name:
**the umbrella admission**.

Why now is the same answer as last time, sharpened: the audited sentence is
load-bearing in a published note; the threats it dismisses are being made this
year, against the war this folder already judged; and the treaty floor under the
whole subject just fell out. A debt with interest accruing is best paid early.

## Conjectures

Seven verdicts compete, and — a first for this repository — none of them is mine
going in. The Ukraine note entered its conjectures with a favorite; this one
enters them owing money.

- **Conjecture A — Never yield.** Yielding to nuclear compellence is always
  wrong, everywhere, for everyone. The argument is the incentive cascade: a world
  in which nuclear threats extract concessions is a world that immediately fills
  with nuclear threats — each success writes the next demand's business case, and
  the only stable number of successful nuclear extortions is zero. The base rate
  backs the norm: compellent nuclear threats have almost never worked, precisely
  because targets have mostly refused to let them (the record is in The
  question). At full strength, A goes further: even *entering* the threat into
  your deliberation is a partial payment — the correct calculus treats the
  blackmailer's warhead as if it were not on the table, because the moment it
  purchases attention it has begun to purchase outcomes.

- **Conjecture B — The tail dominates.** Sometimes yielding is right, and this
  war may be such a time. However small the probability of nuclear use, the harm
  term is so large that expected-value arithmetic swamps any finite stake — a few
  oblasts, even a country's full sovereignty, against a nonzero chance of
  megadeaths. Refusing to run that arithmetic is not integrity; it is innumeracy
  wearing it. And escalation is not fully controlled by either side — the threat
  that leaves something to chance [VERIFY: Schelling] means resistance itself
  rolls dice for millions who never agreed to play. The restraint the West
  actually practiced was, on B's reading, morally correct — and more of it would
  have been more correct.

- **Conjecture C — There is no line.** The Ukraine note's sentence assumed risk
  management ("how") and yielding ("whether") can be kept apart. They cannot.
  Every "how" restraint of this war delivered part of what the threats demanded:
  the no-fly zone never came, the deep-strike permissions arrived years late, the
  troops never arrived at all — each a purchased outcome, paid in the currency
  the blackmailer named. "Managing risk" and "partially capitulating" are one set
  of acts under two descriptions, and no principled stopping point separates
  reasonable caution from de facto veto. If C stands, the audited sentence
  defaults — there is no line to defend, only a slope with better and worse
  footing.

- **Conjecture D — Build the counter-structure.** The yield-or-resist dilemma is
  a symptom of prior failure, and moralizing about it mid-crisis is triage
  dressed as philosophy. What matters is making nuclear coercion structurally
  non-viable before it is tried: extended deterrence credible enough to
  pre-commit the answer, automatic tripwires that remove the coercer's hope of a
  decision to intimidate, assurances with enforcement — everything the Budapest
  Memorandum was not. Where a nuclear threat is credible enough to pose this
  note's question, somebody already failed years earlier — and the moral weight
  sits there, on the umbrella states between crises, not on the defender inside
  one.

- **Conjecture E — Better red than dead.** The honest capitulationist. Strip away
  B's probability apparatus and say it plainly: survival lexically outranks
  freedom. A subjugated nation can resist later — culturally, demographically,
  politically; an incinerated one cannot do anything at all. Every value the
  defense protects presupposes survivors to hold it; when annihilation is
  genuinely on the table, no quantity of sovereignty buys back the wager. The
  slogan was meant as an insult; E wears it as arithmetic-free common sense:
  first remain, then be free.

- **Conjecture F — The taboo is the shield.** What actually protects the world is
  neither resolve nor arithmetic but an eighty-year-old norm: nuclear weapons are
  not used, not usable, not weighed [VERIFY: Tannenwald]. The taboo does its work
  only while the weapons stay outside ordinary calculation — and both sides of
  this note's dilemma corrode it: B, by pricing the unthinkable into policy; A
  and D, by building postures that treat use as a countable move. F's counsel:
  answer nuclear threats with studied refusal to treat them as moves in a game at
  all — neither pay nor price; starve the category.

- **Conjecture G — Supreme emergency.** The rules hold — until the heavens are
  genuinely about to fall, and then they bend [VERIFY: Walzer]. G is the voice
  that licenses exceptions on both sides of the table: a defender may do the
  otherwise-impermissible when facing extinction, and a community may yield the
  otherwise-unyieldable when the threat is real, imminent, and existential.
  Everything turns on keeping "supreme emergency" a narrow gate rather than a
  rhetorical device — which is exactly what every blackmailer will contest.
