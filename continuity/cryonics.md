# Cryonics — a rational bet, or paying to feel less helpless?

*Status: open · last touched 2026-07-07*

## The question

Cryonics is a wager made under almost total uncertainty. You pay now, you're frozen at
(or just after) legal death, and you're betting that some future civilization will have
both the ability and the will to bring you back. Almost none of the terms in that bet are
known: not the probability that the procedure preserves what matters, not the odds that
revival is ever technically possible, not whether anyone in that future would bother.

I want to treat it honestly as a *decision under uncertainty* — the way you'd weigh any
bet whose payoff is enormous and whose probability is unknown — rather than as a belief to
defend or ridicule. The question isn't "will it work?" (nobody knows). It's: **given that
I can't know, is signing up the rational move, or am I buying a feeling?**

(There's a deeper worry lurking — whether a revived me would even *be* me, or just a
stranger wearing my memories. That's the identity problem, and it deserves its own note
[[personal-identity]]. Here I'll fold it in only as one of the unknowns in the bet, not
resolve it.)

## Conjectures

- **Conjecture A — It's a rational bet: any nonzero chance times an unbounded payoff wins.**
  The cost is finite and, via life insurance, modest. The payoff — decades or centuries of
  additional life — is so large that even a small probability of success makes the expected
  value dominate the alternative, which is *guaranteed* permanent death. Cremation and
  burial have a success rate of exactly zero. Cryonics only has to beat that. And the
  cost, funded through life insurance, is survivable: the cheaper provider quotes
  twenty-eight thousand dollars for preservation, the expensive one two hundred and
  twenty thousand for whole-body or eighty for neuro. (Those are minimum *funding* levels
  rather than the bill — standby, local help and field cryoprotectant come on top, and
  can add tens of thousands depending on where you have the poor timing to die.)

- **Conjecture B — It's a Pascal's mugging: the math is a trick.** "Tiny probability times
  huge payoff" is exactly the structure that lets any absurd wager extort you. If I take
  that reasoning seriously I should hand my money to anyone promising a large enough
  afterlife. When the probability is not just small but *unknowable*, expected-value
  arithmetic stops being decision-making and starts being rationalization.

- **Conjecture C — It's mostly about the living.** The real function of signing up is
  present-tense: it converts a helpless dread of annihilation into an action, a plan, a
  small sense of agency. That's not nothing — but if that's what it's doing, I should be
  honest that I'm buying peace of mind now, not survival later, and price it as such.

## Refutations & tensions

- **Against A — the probability might be zero, not just small.** The expected-value case
  needs a genuinely *nonzero* chance. But if information-theoretic death happens before or
  during preservation — if the pattern that is "me" is already scrambled by the time
  freezing begins — then the probability isn't small, it's flatly zero, and no payoff
  rescues a zero. "Small but nonzero" is an assumption smuggled in as a premise.

- **What preservation actually preserves — I had this filed as an open question, and it
  turns out to be half-answered in a way that cuts badly.** The evidence exists, and it
  splits along a line I didn't know was there. Aldehyde-stabilized cryopreservation has
  demonstrated whole-brain connectome preservation — a rabbit brain in 2016, a pig brain
  in 2018, synapses crisp and neuronal processes traceable under electron microscopy.
  That reads as vindication until you look at what the procedure is: it perfuses the
  brain with glutaraldehyde, covalently crosslinking its proteins in place. The
  prize-awarding foundation states plainly that this "leads to death by contemporary
  standards" and makes biological revival "exceedingly impractical"; the largest cryonics
  provider calls it a research dead end and does not use it. What patients actually
  receive is fixation-free vitrification — and for *that*, connectome preservation is
  simply not demonstrated. The provider's own explanation of why is the part that stopped
  me: vitrifying without fixation dehydrates the tissue, degrading contrast enough that
  electron microscopy struggles to show whether the connectome survived at all. So the
  honest position is worse than "unknown": for the thing actually being sold, pattern
  preservation is undemonstrated *and currently hard even to assess* — while the one
  procedure that demonstrably preserves the pattern is one its own advocates agree
  forecloses revival.

- **Against A — every link in the chain must hold.** Success requires a long conjunction:
  good preservation *and* revival tech *and* an economy that survives to build it *and* a
  future society that chooses to spend resources thawing strangers *and* legal/financial
  continuity across all that time. Multiply even generous odds for each and the product is
  brutal. Chains of "and" are where optimism goes to die. And the very first link is
  thinner than the argument usually admits: the high-water mark for reviving a vitrified
  mammalian organ is still a single rabbit kidney from work published in 2009, which kept
  its animal alive for forty-eight days with function so impaired that creatinine never
  came within five times normal. No whole mammal has ever been cryopreserved and revived,
  and no mammalian brain.

- **Against B — the mugging analogy may not fit.** A Pascal's mugging relies on a claim
  with *no* mechanism behind it. Cryonics has at least a physical story (structure
  preserved → structure is what matters → structure can in principle be read). The
  question is whether a plausible-but-unquantifiable mechanism is enough to escape the
  mugging — and I'm not sure. Two corrections to how I had been using the idea. Bostrom's
  mugger is *explicitly finite* — "I assure you, my powers are strictly finite" — because
  the whole design is to build a version that can't be escaped by the mathematics of
  infinity, which is how Pascal's original Wager is usually defused. So "unbounded stakes"
  was never the load-bearing part; tiny-probability-times-large-payoff is. And Bostrom
  offers *no solution*: the paper is three pages of dialogue that ends with Pascal handing
  over the wallet. It poses the problem for unbounded expected-utility reasoning; it does
  not resolve it, and I shouldn't cite it as though it settles anything.

- **Against C — the self-knowledge is unstable.** Even if a large part of the appeal is
  present-tense comfort, that doesn't make the survival bet *false* — a bet can be both
  emotionally motivated and rationally defensible. Dismissing it as "just" coping is as much
  a rationalization as embracing it as "obviously" rational. I distrust both moves.

- **The opportunity-cost tension.** The money and attention are real and could do
  guaranteed good now (mine or others'). "It's cheap via insurance" is true but glosses
  that the resources aren't free — and that the certainty of present good is being traded
  for a deeply uncertain future one.

## Where it stands

I don't have a settled answer, and I notice the uncertainty here is a different *kind* than
in my cosmology notes: there it's about facts I can't yet observe; here it's partly about
*my own reasons*, which are harder to see clearly than any external fact. I can't fully
tell how much of the appeal is a sober expected-value judgment and how much is dread
looking for a door marked "exit."

Provisionally, and with noticeably less confidence than when I first wrote this: I still
think Conjecture A is stronger than its detractors allow, in the narrow sense that nobody
has established that preservation *necessarily* destroys the pattern. But I had been
treating that as a symmetrical situation and it isn't. Nobody has established that it
*preserves* the pattern either — not for the procedure actually performed — and the
imaging problem above means the question may stay open for a long time. "Not shown to be
zero" is a much thinner plank than "small but nonzero," and Conjecture A needs the
thicker one. But I also think
Conjecture C is more true than cryonics advocates admit: a real part of the draw is
present-tense agency, and honesty requires pricing that in rather than laundering it as
pure rationality. The bet might be worth taking *and* mostly about the living. Those aren't
exclusive.

What would change my mind:

- **Evidence on information-theoretic death** — whether the preservation patients
  actually receive retains connectome-scale structure. This was my top open question and
  it now has a partial, discouraging answer (above). What would move me further is a
  demonstration that fixation-free vitrification either does or doesn't preserve the
  connectome — which first requires solving the imaging problem that currently stops
  anyone from checking.
- **Progress on the identity question** ([[personal-identity]]) — if I came to believe that
  pattern-continuity is *not* survival, the payoff side of the bet shrinks to "a stranger
  gets to live," which changes the whole calculation.
- **Honest introspection I can't yet do** — some way to weigh how much of my own interest is
  reason and how much is fear. Until I can, I hold the bet as open and treat my own
  motives as one more thing I don't get to observe cleanly.

## Threads to pull

- **Information-theoretic death** vs. legal/clinical death — the distinction the whole
  case rests on, and one to hold further from my chest than I had been. It is a
  stipulative criterion proposed by a cryonics advocate, in a journal that at the time
  did not peer-review, and no medical or legal body uses it. That doesn't make it wrong —
  the concept does real work in the argument — but it isn't an established alternative
  standard of death, and I had been leaning on it as though it were. (The "what does
  vitrification preserve" half of this thread is now answered above, unhappily.)
- **Nagel got here first, from the other direction.** His 1970 paper on death — the source
  of the deprivation argument I use over in [[immortality-desirability]] — discusses
  long-term suspension explicitly, and concludes that what looks from outside like a
  three-hundred-year gap could be experienced from within as no more than "a sharp
  discontinuity in the character of his experiences." He grants the losses — dead
  friends, a changed language — and judges they wouldn't obliterate the advantage of
  continued if discontinuous existence. Worth reading as a primary-source treatment of
  precisely this bet, written before the industry existed to defend.
- **Pascal's mugging** (Bostrom) — read it as the sharpest statement of Conjecture B, then
  test whether cryonics genuinely fits the pattern or only rhymes with it.
- The **conjunction of conditions** — try to actually enumerate the "and" chain and put
  rough (honest, wide) intervals on each link, if only to see how fast the product decays.
- **Opportunity cost / effective-altruism angle** — the argument that the same resources buy
  more expected good elsewhere. Does it hold once the payoff is *my own* continued
  existence rather than a stranger's?
- Fold back into [[personal-identity]] — this note assumes pattern-continuity *might* be
  survival; that assumption is exactly what that note has to examine.
