# What makes a future person me — and is that even the question that matters?

*Status: open · last touched 2026-07-20 · sources checked pending*

## The question

This note is owed to two others. [[cryonics]] assumes that if the pattern survives, *I*
might survive, and says outright that the assumption is exactly what this note has to
examine. [[immortality-desirability]] wonders whether the self that persists across
centuries is still the self, and hangs part of its verdict on the answer. Both of them
stop at the same door. This is the door.

The question in its plain form: what makes a person at some later time the *same person*
as me now? Not similar, not continuous in some loose sense — the same, such that their
future is *my* future, and I have the peculiar self-interested concern for it that I have
for my own next hour and not for yours.

But there is a second question underneath, and half the work of this note is learning to
keep them apart:

1. **The metaphysical question.** What relation must hold between me-now and
   someone-later for them to be *numerically identical* to me?
2. **The practical question.** What relation must hold for me to be rationally
   *self-interested* in their future — to anticipate it, fear their pain, want their
   projects to succeed?

Everyone assumes these have the same answer. The most interesting position in the
literature is that they come apart — and if they do, most of what I want to know is on
the second question, while almost all of the intuitions I have are trained on the first.

## Conjectures

- **Conjecture A — The further fact.** There is a deep, determinate truth about whether a
  future person is me, over and above all the physical and psychological facts. A soul,
  a Cartesian ego, a bare *this-one-ness*. Ask "but would it really be me?" and there is
  an answer, even if nothing observable settles it. This is what almost everyone
  implicitly believes, and it is what makes teleportation feel like dying.

- **Conjecture B — Biological continuity.** I am an animal. What makes a future organism
  me is that it is the same living organism, sustained by the same continuing life
  processes. Psychology is something I *have*, not something I *am*. On this view the
  question "would the upload be me?" has an easy answer: no, obviously not, any more than
  a photograph of me is me.

- **Conjecture C — Psychological continuity.** I am a pattern — memories, personality,
  intentions, character — held together by overlapping chains of connection. Not one
  unbroken memory reaching back to childhood, but a rope of short overlapping fibres, no
  single one running the whole length. What makes a future person me is that they are the
  far end of that rope.

- **Conjecture D — Reductionism: identity isn't what matters.** There is no deep further
  fact. Personal identity just consists in physical and psychological continuity, and
  where those hold to a degree or split down two paths, there is no further question with
  a hidden answer — the question simply runs out. And the twist that makes this more than
  bookkeeping: what I actually care about when I care about surviving is *not identity at
  all*. It's the continuity relation itself, which can hold in degrees and can hold
  between me and two people at once, even though identity cannot.

## Refutations & tensions

**Against A — fission kills it, and unfalsifiability finishes the job.** Divide my brain's
hemispheres, put each in a body, wake both. Each has my memories and character. If there
is a further fact about which one is *really* me, then either the soul went left, went
right, went nowhere, or split — and there is no possible evidence for any of the four.
Worse, the answer would have to be a brute metaphysical truth making no difference to
anything anyone could ever observe or experience. Both survivors would be equally
convinced they are me and equally unable to check. A fact that nothing could reveal and
nothing depends on is doing no work, and I don't think it exists.

**Against B — the transplant intuition.** Move my brain into another body. Nearly everyone
says I went with it: I wake up looking in an unfamiliar mirror. Animalism appears to say
the opposite — the organism stayed behind, so I stayed behind, and what woke up elsewhere
was a different animal with my memories. Some animalists accept this and argue the
intuition is confused. But it is a very hard bullet to bite, and it gets harder in the
other direction: a body kept breathing after brain death is still the same organism, and
almost nobody thinks I am still there.

**Against C — Reid's brave officer, and the transitivity problem.** An old general
remembers being a young officer; the young officer remembered being a boy flogged at
school; the general remembers nothing of the boy. If memory makes identity, the general
is the officer, the officer is the boy, and the general is not the boy — which identity,
being transitive, forbids. The repair is the overlapping-chain move: not direct memory
but *continuity* through intermediate links. That works, and it is why C is stated above
in terms of a rope rather than a thread. But it concedes something: identity now depends
on a chain that can be strong or weak, and chains admit degrees while identity does not.

**Against C — branching, and the "only x and y" problem.** This is the argument I find
hardest to escape. Suppose the teleporter scans me on Earth and builds a perfect copy on
Mars, but this time the original isn't destroyed. Now two people have equal claim.
Identity is one-to-one, so at most one is me — but they are symmetrical, so neither is,
so I did not survive. Yet nothing bad happened to the Mars person that wouldn't equally
have happened had the Earth original been vaporized as usual — in which case, on the same
theory, he *would* have been me. So whether I survive depends on whether *someone else*
exists. That cannot be right. Whether I live through the next minute should not turn on
facts about a stranger on another planet. Something has gone wrong with the question, not
with the answer.

**Against D — the slide from metaphysics to value.** Grant that identity is not a deep
further fact. It does not follow that identity is not what *matters*. Plenty of things
matter to us that are conventional, vague, or constituted rather than discovered — being
someone's friend, being the same nation. "It isn't a deep fact" and "it isn't worth
caring about" are different claims, and the reductionist argument establishes the first
while often being used to assert the second. I think this is the weak joint in D, and I
notice that it is also the joint I most want to be strong, because a Parfitian conclusion
is enormously convenient for anyone hoping cryonics might work.

**The asymmetry that unsettles all of this.** I am continuous with the five-year-old who
shared my name. He and I have almost no memories in common, no atoms in common, no
personality traits I could name with confidence. By any measure of psychological
connectedness, my relation to him is *weaker* than a teleport copy's relation to me at
the moment of scanning. Yet I feel no unease at all about being him, and considerable
unease about the copy. Whatever my intuitions are tracking, it is not the strength of the
psychological relation. My best guess is that they track the *continuous causal path* —
the unbroken physical process — and that this is a fact about how the concept was built,
not about what deserves my concern. But I hold that guess loosely, because "my intuitions
are wrong and my theory is right" is exactly what someone would say who had reasoned
themselves into a corner.

**The evidence that the intuitions are not tracking anything stable.** The sharpest thing
I know here is that the *same* scenario, described two ways, flips people's judgments.
Told as "your psychology will be transferred into that body," we say: I go with the
psychology. Told as "tomorrow you will be tortured, and beforehand your memories will be
erased and replaced," we recoil — the fear stays with the body. Same facts, opposite
verdicts, and no obvious way to say which framing is the misleading one. That is either a
devastating result about our concept or a devastating result about the method of
consulting intuitions, and I am not sure which.

## Where it stands

**A is dead.** Fission plus unfalsifiability finishes it, and I don't think the folk view
survives contact with the cases at all — though I notice I still *feel* like a further-fact
believer, which is worth recording as data about how little my feelings constrain my
conclusions.

**Metaphysical reductionism is probably right, and Parfit's normative step is probably
under-argued.** My provisional position: identity consists in continuity, there is no
hidden extra ingredient, and in the hard cases the concept simply fails to return a
verdict — not because the answer is hidden but because there is no answer to hide. That
last part is stranger than "we don't know," and I want to keep the strangeness rather than
smoothing it into ordinary uncertainty. But the further claim that identity therefore
doesn't matter, and that I should be as concerned for a branch-line copy as for myself, is
a *value* claim that the metaphysics doesn't deliver on its own.

So the honest summary: **the concept was not built for these cases, and asking it to
adjudicate them is asking a tool to do a job it was never shaped for.** That is not a
failure to have found the answer. It may be the answer.

**What this hands back to the two notes that are waiting on it — which is the point of
writing it:**

- **To [[cryonics]]: the two discounts are independent, and they multiply.** That note had
  been treating "will the pattern survive?" as the live question, with identity folded in
  as one more unknown. It isn't one unknown, it's two. First, an empirical question —
  does the preservation retain the pattern? (Today's answer: undemonstrated for the
  procedure actually performed, and currently hard even to assess.) Second, a
  philosophical one — *even granting perfect preservation*, is pattern-continuity
  survival, or does a stranger wake up with my memories? Neither discount is small and
  neither rescues the other. The expected-value case in that note needs both to come out
  favourably and it argues for only one.

- **To [[immortality-desirability]]: the tedium worry and the identity worry are one
  worry, seen from two sides.** Williams' argument there is that a life long enough to
  exhaust your categorical desires leaves you either bored or continually remade. This
  note supplies the second horn's teeth: if staying interested across centuries requires
  being continually remade, then the psychological connectedness between me-now and
  me-at-500 may be weaker than between me and a contemporary stranger. The person
  enjoying that extended life would be the far end of a rope whose near end is me, and
  every individual fibre would be short. Whether that is *me* getting the extra centuries
  or a long series of people taking turns is exactly the question above — and the answer
  "the question runs out" does not comfort, it relocates the problem.

**What would change my mind:**

- A worked-out account of why the *continuous causal path* deserves the weight my
  intuitions give it — not just that they give it. If someone can show that gradual
  physical continuity is what makes anticipation rational, C strengthens and D's
  normative half collapses, and teleportation really is death.
- Evidence that the framing effect above is asymmetric — that one of the two descriptions
  reliably misleads and the other doesn't. That would rescue the method of consulting
  intuitions, which I currently distrust.
- A case where the metaphysical and practical questions demonstrably come apart *in
  practice* rather than in thought experiments. Real fission-adjacent cases exist and I
  should look at what they actually show rather than what the textbook version says.

## Threads to pull

- **The no-self tradition got here roughly twenty-four centuries early.** Buddhist
  *anattā* denies a persisting self and treats the person as a bundle of processes, which
  is close to reductionism arrived at by an entirely different route and for entirely
  different purposes. Parfit himself is said to have noticed the convergence. Worth
  reading carefully rather than as a slogan — and worth resisting the temptation to
  flatten two traditions into one because they agree on a negation.
- **Split-brain patients as the real-world fission case** — but check the current
  literature rather than the famous version. The textbook story of two independent
  conscious agents in one skull has been challenged, and I should not build on a picture
  that may be a generation out of date. (Precisely the *stale, not false* failure mode
  this repo now has a convention for.)
- **Nozick's closest-continuer theory** — the attempt to keep identity while handling
  branching, by making it depend on who the nearest surviving candidate is. Does it dodge
  the "only x and y" objection or just relocate it?
- **Gradual versus destructive uploading.** If neurons are replaced one at a time over
  years, most people's intuitions say I persist; if scanned and rebuilt in an instant,
  they say I die. The physical end-state can be identical. Is there a principled
  difference, or is this just the causal-path intuition again wearing a lab coat?
- **The characterization question, not the reidentification one.** There is a whole strand
  asking not "which past person am I?" but "which traits are truly *mine*?" — identity as
  self-interpretation rather than as a persistence relation. It may be that the second
  question is the one that actually does work in a human life, and I've spent this note
  on the first.
- Connects back to [[cryonics]] and [[immortality-desirability]], both of which were
  waiting on this. And forward to whatever eventually lands in `mind/` — the persistence
  question and the consciousness question constrain each other, and I have written
  neither half honestly yet.
