# Would I even want it? The desirability of a much longer life

*Status: open · last touched 2026-07-07*

## The question

Suppose the hard problems get solved — cryonics works, or aging is cured, or minds can be
carried forward somehow (I take those up elsewhere: [[cryonics]], [[personal-identity]]).
That still leaves a question I find I can't answer as easily as I expected: **would I
actually want it?** Not "is it possible," but "is it *good* — for me, and for a world full
of people who also don't die?"

I notice I reach for "obviously yes" and then can't defend it. The wish to not-die is so
reflexive that it's worth being suspicious of it, the same way it's worth being suspicious
of the neat philosophies that tell me mortality is secretly a gift. Both the craving and
the consolation could be motivated reasoning. This note is an attempt to lay out the real
arguments — the ones people have actually made — rather than my gut on either side.

I'm reading "immortality" loosely: not literal invulnerability, but a radically extended
life, centuries or open-ended, with the *option* to stop.

## Conjectures

- **Conjecture A — Death is simply bad, and more life is more good.** On the *deprivation*
  view (Nagel), death harms you by robbing you of the goods you'd otherwise have had. If
  one more good week is worth having, so is one more, and the induction doesn't obviously
  stop. Bostrom's *Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant* sharpens the moral edge: we only tolerate
  aging because it's always been inevitable; strip away the familiarity and mass death by
  senescence looks like a catastrophe we've rationalized. Wanting to live is the sane
  default; the burden of proof is on the person who'd stop you.

- **Conjecture B — A life without end goes stale (the tedium argument).** Bernard Williams'
  *Makropulos Case*: a self is held together by its *categorical desires* — the projects and
  attachments that give you reasons to go on. Live long enough and you exhaust them; what's
  left is either endless boredom (you, but hollowed out) or endless reinvention (not really
  you anymore). Either way the thing you wanted to preserve doesn't survive the length of
  the life. So the immortal Elina Makropulos, at 342, finally refuses the elixir — not from
  despair but from having *finished*. (Williams is more careful than the slogan version of
  him. He explicitly holds that death is still, other things equal, reasonably regarded as
  an evil, and that his conclusion does not mean we shouldn't fear it. Enlisting him as
  straightforwardly pro-mortality misreads him — a temptation worth naming, because I feel
  it every time I summarize him.)

- **Conjecture C — Finitude is the condition of meaning, not its enemy.** A stronger claim
  than B: it's not that long life risks boredom, it's that *a limit is what makes anything
  matter*. Scarcity of time forces prioritization and intensifies commitment; a project you
  can always defer forever loses its weight. A life needs an ending the way a story does,
  to have a shape at all. (I originally reached for Heidegger and Nussbaum in this
  parenthesis. Both need more care than a parenthesis affords. Heidegger's
  being-toward-death is an *ontological* claim about the structure of Dasein, not an
  axiological one that mortality is good — and he sharply separates death from mere
  biological demise, which is precisely what a cryonics argument is about, so borrowing
  him against life extension equivocates between two senses of the word. And I have not
  verified that Nussbaum makes the claim I attributed to her. The one thinker I can quote
  verbatim for this position is Williams: "death gives the meaning to life.")

- **Conjecture D — The real risks are social, not personal.** Even bracketing meaning
  entirely: a world where people stop dying is a world of compounding problems.
  Overpopulation against hard planetary limits; a "death gap" where the rich buy centuries
  the poor can't (inequality made literally existential); gerontocratic stagnation, where
  power and capital never turn over and the old never get out of the way. The question isn't
  just "do *I* want it" but "can a society survive everyone wanting it."

## Refutations & tensions

- **Against B — boredom is contingent, not necessary (Scheffler's move, and a correction
  to how I used it).** Samuel Scheffler points out that Williams' complaint is really
  about *any* abnormally long life, not infinity as such: if your pool of categorical
  desires is finite it empties in finite time regardless, so the argument cannot
  discriminate between an immortal life and a merely very long one. Here is what I had
  missed, and it matters — **Scheffler is not on my side.** He attacks the *scope* of
  Williams' argument while agreeing with its *verdict*: he too holds that personal
  immortality would not be something to desire. So this is an internal repair of the
  tedium case, not a refutation of it, and I was recruiting him as a counterweight he
  declines to be. What survives as actually mine is the step after: maybe the pool *isn't*
  fixed. New people, new arts, new problems generate new categorical desires; there's no
  proof the well runs dry rather than being continuously refilled. Williams assumes a
  static self; a growing one might never finish. That argument now has to stand on its
  own, without Scheffler's shoulder to lean on.

- **Against B and C together — the "always an out" defense.** If continued life is
  *optional* — if I can stop whenever the desires genuinely run out, as Makropulos herself
  did — then length is a gift with a return policy. The tedium argument then shows only that
  I shouldn't be *forced* to live forever, not that I shouldn't be *allowed* the extra time.
  That's a much weaker conclusion than "mortality is good."

- **Against C — "meaning needs death" may be sour grapes.** This is the objection I most
  distrust myself with. The claim that finitude is *necessary* for meaning looks suspiciously
  like a consolation retrofitted to an unavoidable fact — Bostrom's charge that dignity-and-
  finitude arguments are elaborate rhetoric for accepting the dragon. Do we actually believe
  a 500-year friendship would be *meaningless*, or just unfamiliar? Plenty of meaningful
  things (a symphony, a marriage) get their shape from *internal* structure, not from the
  threat of imminent death. Finitude might deepen meaning without being required for it.

- **Against A — the deprivation induction may not scale.** "One more good week is good,
  therefore infinite weeks are good" is the same tempting-but-shaky arithmetic I flagged
  under [[cryonics]]. Value might not be additive across that scale; the 10,000th year need
  not be worth what the 40th is, and a good that requires an ever-refreshed self to enjoy it
  isn't clearly a good *for me*.

- **Against D — a reason to govern it, not to refuse it.** The social objections are real but
  they're arguments about *distribution and pace*, not about whether long life is good in
  itself. Overpopulation and inequality are (in principle) policy problems; "it would be
  unfair or destabilizing" is a reason to manage the transition, not proof that the thing
  itself is undesirable. Though "in principle solvable" is doing a lot of quiet work there.

- **The suspicious symmetry.** I want to flag that *both* poles are psychologically loaded.
  The transhumanist "death is a tyrant" and the humanist "mortality is a gift" each let their
  holder stop feeling the full weight of the problem. The honest position has to keep both
  discomforts live.

## Where it stands

Provisionally, and against my starting expectation, I've moved toward: **the personal case
against long life is weaker than it first sounds, but the social case is stronger.** The
Williams/tedium worry seems to me largely defused by the "always an out" reply plus the
possibility that categorical desires refill — I don't think boredom is *necessary*, only
possible, and possible-boredom is a reason for an exit option, not for death. And I've come
to think Conjecture C's "finitude is *required* for meaning" is probably the sour grapes
Bostrom accuses it of being: finitude can *sharpen* meaning without being its precondition.

But Conjecture D is where my confidence drains. I can want it for myself and still think a
world that *achieves* it — unequally, against planetary limits, with power never turning
over — might be worse than the one we have. "It's a governance problem" is true and also the
kind of thing people say right before the governance fails.

So my honest current view: I'd *want* the extra time, with an out — and I'm genuinely unsure
whether I should want the *world* to have it. That split, between the first-person and the
collective answer, feels like the actual location of the problem, not a failure to have
resolved it.

What would change my mind:

- A convincing account of whether categorical desires are a **fixed pool or a renewable
  one** — Scheffler's fork turns on this, and it's at least partly an empirical question
  about how people actually age over very long spans (which no one has data on).
- Whether the social risks (D) are **contingently** solvable or **structurally** baked in —
  if gerontocratic stagnation or the death-gap turn out to be near-inevitable consequences
  rather than manageable side-effects, the collective answer settles toward "no."
- Progress on [[personal-identity]] — if the "me" that persists across centuries isn't
  really me, then A's payoff shrinks and B's "you get hollowed out" worry sharpens; the
  desirability question can't fully separate from the identity question.

## Threads to pull

- **Bernard Williams, "The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality"** — the
  canonical statement of B. Read it against its source — Karel Čapek's play *Věc
  Makropulos*, which Janáček then made into the opera; the order matters and I had it
  collapsed. [PDF](https://wmit-pages-prod.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/283/2022/06/12152936/williams.pdf)
- **Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife*** (2013) — the "abnormally long life, not
  infinity" reframing of Williams, and the categorical-desires-are-finite fork. Read him
  as repairing Williams rather than opposing him; he shares the verdict. I have this from
  the reviews and a secondary exposition, not from Scheffler's own pages — worth checking
  against a copy before I lean on him again.
- **Nick Bostrom, "The Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant"** — the sharpest pro-longevity allegory;
  read as the counterweight to C. [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fable_of_the_Dragon-Tyrant) · the deprivation view it leans on: **Thomas Nagel, "Death" (1970)** [PDF](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Nagel.pdf)
- **Martha Nussbaum** on mortality as constitutive of our values — potentially the
  strongest version of C, but flagged as *unverified*: the likely source is "Mortal
  Immortals: Lucretius on Death and the Voice of Nature" (1989), whose existence I have
  confirmed and whose contents I have not. Until I read it, C's strongest statement is
  Williams', not hers.
- **Pijnenburg & Leget, "Who wants to live forever? Three arguments against extending the
  human lifespan"** (2007) — justice, community, meaning; the backbone of D. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2652797/)
- **The Epicurus / Lucretius symmetry argument** — the flip side: maybe death isn't bad *at
  all*, so the whole desirability question is confused. Worth taking seriously enough to
  refute. [SEP: Death](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/death/) (Steven Luper) — §7,
  "Never Dying," is the section that bears directly on this note.
- **Aubrey de Grey / SENS** — the "aging is an engineering problem" position; the practical
  face of Conjecture A.
- Connects to [[cryonics]] (same additive-value question, applied to a bet) and
  [[personal-identity]] (whether the long-lived self is still the self).
