# Is science just another religion?

*Status: open · last touched 2026-08-31 · sources checked 2026-08-31*

## The question

Prompted by a Facebook exchange. I had argued that a biblical prophecy fails a checkable test, and the reply was not about the prophecy but about me: "Why hide behind atheism? Your god is 'science' — read: corrupt pseudoscience. It is a false god that has failed. I believe in the real God, who does not fail" (my translation from Czech), attached to a meme in which a public-health official is drawn as a clown under four captions: *trust the science — sometimes science is a little wrong — we made science up entirely — science refuses to testify.* The move is common enough to deserve its own note. It reframes an argument about evidence as a contest between two faiths, and once that frame holds no evidence can matter, because both sides are just believers.

So the question is not whether scientists are honest or whether institutions overclaim (some aren't; some do). It is narrower: is the relationship between a reasonable person and science *the same kind of thing* as the relationship between a believer and their religion? And if not, where exactly is the difference — stated precisely enough that a critic could show me I am wrong?

A real answer has to survive the strongest form of the charge, which is not the meme but the philosophical version: that science rests on unprovable presuppositions, that laypeople accept it on authority, that its institutions punish heretics, and that science too rescues its theories from refutation by adjusting them. All four have something to them.

## Conjectures

- **Conjecture A — Scientism is a religion in all but name.** It has priests (experts), scripture (peer-reviewed journals), rituals of deference ("trust the science"), heretics who are cast out, moral claims derived from it, and foundational articles of faith it cannot justify — the uniformity of nature, the reliability of reason, the assumption that the world is intelligible. The layperson's "the science says" is functionally identical to "the Bible says": a claim accepted because of who said it.
- **Conjecture B — Science is the opposite of religion.** Religion is belief without evidence, science is belief proportioned to evidence; one is dogma, the other is method. They are not two species of one genus but contraries.
- **Conjecture C — The method is not a religion, but the relationship to it can be.** What distinguishes science is not what its adherents believe or how confidently, but what happens to disagreement and to error: the final court of appeal is an observation anyone can in principle repeat, publishing an error is permitted and rewarded rather than punished, and the heretic who brings data can win. Whether a given person or institution actually lives by that is a separate, empirical question — and the answer is often no.

## Refutations & tensions

**Against B.** It is too flattering to be true. Most of what I hold about science I hold on authority: I have not measured the age of the universe or replicated a vaccine trial. Nor is "belief without evidence" a fair description of the other side — the Bible's own definition of faith calls it *evidence*. In the Bible 21 translation my opponent uses, Hebrews 11:1 reads "Víra je podstata věcí, v něž doufáme, důkaz skutečností, jež nevidíme" — faith is the substance of things hoped for, the *proof* of realities unseen. The believer is not claiming to believe without evidence; they are claiming that faith is a kind of evidence. B as stated cannot answer that, and I had to weaken my first draft here. The real objection is not that faith supplies no evidence but that it supplies evidence that cannot fail — see the dial, below.

**Against A — the presuppositions argument.** Yes, science assumes that nature is regular and that tomorrow's experiment will behave like today's, and Hume's point that this cannot be established without circularity stands (I cite it from memory; the *Enquiry*, section IV, was not re-read for this note). I do not have an answer to the strong, presuppositionalist form of this argument — the one that grants the assumption is universal and then asks which worldview can ground it — and I should say so rather than pretend. What I can say is narrower: every human being, the critic included, relies on the same assumption every time they cross a bridge, so a presupposition shared by all reasoning cannot make one particular activity a religion. Either it makes everything one, and the word stops discriminating, or the line between science and faith has to be drawn somewhere else. My first draft also claimed that "if regularity failed, instruments would notice" — withdrawn, because noticing a failure of regularity with instruments presupposes the regularity of the instruments.

**Against A — the authority argument.** This is the strongest part of A and it deserves a real concession: the lay relationship to science can indeed be fideistic, and "trust the science" as a slogan often is. The difference is not that I verify everything. It is what happens when *someone* checks and finds a discrepancy. When Barry Marshall and Robin Warren claimed that peptic ulcers were an infection rather than a stress disease, they were contradicting the consensus of their field; the Nobel Assembly's own press release says they "challenged prevailing dogmas" and "made an irrefutable case", and the prize followed — decades after the first observation, which matters below. The system's incentives eventually rewarded the heretic with data. Compare a system in which the same claim is offered as evidence when it appears to come true and declared beyond the reach of evidence when it does not. Deference to a process without that asymmetry is not the same act as deference to one with it, even when both feel like trust from the inside.

**Against A — the corruption argument ("corrupt pseudoscience").** The replication crisis is real and I should not pretend otherwise: when the Open Science Collaboration re-ran 100 published psychology studies, 97% of the originals had reported statistically significant results and 36% of the replications did, with effect sizes roughly halved. Ioannidis had argued in 2005 that most published research findings may be false. During COVID, public-health institutions spoke with more confidence than their data supported, and changes of course were often not acknowledged as such (an impression, not a sourced claim). All of that is fair ammunition — and the 97% figure cuts against my own first draft, which said science "publishes its errors rather than reinterpreting them": a literature in which nearly every published result is positive is a literature in which many errors were never published at all. The defensible claim is weaker: publishing an error is *permitted and rewarded*, and sooner or later someone does it — the replication crisis was discovered, quantified and published by scientists, in science journals, by scientific methods. Notice, too, that the word "pseudoscience" presupposes a standard of real science by which the fake is detected: the critic is borrowing the method's norms to indict the method's institutions. That does not by itself prove the practice is not a faith — reformers have criticized churches from inside with scripture in hand — but it shows the standard in play is the method's own. And the meme's second panel, "sometimes science is a little wrong", can only be drawn because science documents its own errors. Try drawing the same panel for a doctrine that holds its text can never be wrong: there is nothing to put in it, not because there are no errors, but because they are not published as errors. They are reinterpreted.

**The dial — and the objection that science moves it too.** The structure is the same as in the prophecy argument that started the exchange. A claim that is *precise* is checkable and can fail; a claim that is *loose* cannot fail and therefore cannot count as evidence for anything. When a prediction misses and the claim is saved by loosening it — "it was meant spiritually", "not yet" — each move that saves the claim from being false costs exactly as much evidential force as it saves. The strongest reply is that science does exactly this: Uranus misbehaved and Newton was saved by postulating Neptune; galaxies rotate wrongly and gravity is saved by postulating dark matter (both from general knowledge, unsourced here). Duhem and Quine are right that no hypothesis is tested alone, so the dial is never a clean falsification. What distinguishes the two cases is what the move *produces*. Postulating Neptune produced a new, risky prediction — a planet at a stated position, which was found; dark matter is still on probation precisely because its own predictions are still being tested. "It was meant spiritually" produces no new prediction at all; it only explains why the last one did not count. That is Lakatos's line between a progressive and a degenerating adjustment, and it is the line the treatment-of-error argument actually needs. Without it, the dial does not separate science from faith; with it, it does — and it also indicts any scientific program that only absorbs anomalies.

**Where the pragmatic argument is legitimate and where it is cheap.** "You typed that on a phone" is a cheap shot, because the believer never denied that engineering works. The legitimate version answers only the words "a false god that has failed": a method whose predictions hold regardless of what anyone believes about them has not failed in the sense the critic needs. Relativity (special and general effects combined) predicts that GPS satellite clocks gain about 38 microseconds a day on ground clocks, and the system is built around that correction; omit it and position errors would grow by roughly 10 km per day. The satellites do not care whether the engineer is an atheist. That is the whole point, and it is a small one.

**Tension in C.** C says the distinguishing mark is what happens to the heretic with data — but the history of science has heretics with data who were ignored for years or died unheard (Semmelweis), Marshall and Warren waited decades, and religions rehabilitate their heretics too, eventually. So the test cannot be *whether* the heretic is ever vindicated; it has to be about speed and mechanism — how fast, and through what channel, a demonstrated discrepancy reaches the consensus. C is a claim about incentives and tendencies, not about outcomes in every case. That is weaker than I would like, and it is where I would expect a good critic to press. A second tension: the question asks about the *relationship*, and C answers with a property of the *object* (the system corrects itself) — and the believer makes the symmetrical move ("my trust is warranted because my God is real"). The relationship-level mark has to be added separately: conditional trust with a stated exit condition.

## Where it stands

**C, in the weakened form the tensions leave standing.** Science is not a religion, but not for the reason B gives; the difference is not confidence, nor evidence in the abstract, but the treatment of error — as a tendency built into the incentives, not a guarantee in every case. The question that separates the two is not "what do you believe?" but "what would make you say you were wrong — and how fast, and by what mechanism, is the person who shows it heard?" A system that answers the first half with a checkable condition, and whose adjustments after a miss produce new risky predictions rather than merely absorbing the miss, is on the science side of the line, whatever it calls itself; a system that cannot answer the first half is on the other side, whatever it calls itself. By that test some of what circulates under the name of science — including some institutional "trust us" — is on the wrong side too, and the critic is right to say so. And on the person's side, the mark of a non-religious relationship to science is not how much one has verified but whether one's trust is conditional with the condition stated — which is what the list below is for.

The critic's own scripture, read in context, is closer to that test than to the meme: "Všechno prověřujte; dobrého se držte" — test everything, hold on to what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21, Bible 21). The preceding verse is "Proroctvími nepohrdejte" — do not despise prophecies — so the instruction is specifically about testing prophecies, which is the use I am making of it; the believer will reply that the test meant is spiritual discernment, and that reply is available to them.

What would change my mind:

- **Toward A:** a demonstration that the error-treatment difference is illusory — that scientific consensus is, in practice, as immune to disconfirming data as a creed. The replication crisis is not that demonstration, because it was produced from inside. A field that kept a flagship result for a decade after clean, adequately powered, multi-lab failures to replicate — pre-registered or not — without demoting it, would be. So would a showing that the Lakatos line cannot be drawn in practice: that "progressive" and "degenerating" are only ever assigned in hindsight.
- **Toward B:** evidence that the lay relationship to science is in practice mostly calibrated deference rather than tribal badge — survey data showing people update their trust in specific claims when those claims fail. I doubt it; I expect the honest picture is C.

## Threads to pull

- The organized version of A: Feyerabend (*Science in a Free Society*), Midgley (*Science as Salvation*), Polanyi on tacit knowledge and scientific authority; Kuhn on paradigms and what "normal science" does with anomalies. Not yet read for this note; A above is my own reconstruction, and the real thing is probably stronger.
- Lakatos, "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes" — the dial section now leans on his progressive/degenerating distinction from memory; read it properly and check whether the hindsight objection (that the distinction is only ever applied retrospectively) has a good answer.
- Hume on induction and the pragmatic-vindication replies (Reichenbach); the presuppositionalist challenge (which worldview grounds the uniformity of nature) is left unanswered above.
- Survey data on public trust in science (Pew, Eurobarometer): does trust move with track record, or with party?
- The word rendered "důkaz" in Hebrews 11:1 (*elenchos*): what the range of the Greek is, and whether "proof" or "conviction" is the better gloss — it bears on how strong the "faith is evidence" reading is.
