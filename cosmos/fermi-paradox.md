# If others are out there, is the silence an absence — or a choice?

*Status: open · last touched 2026-07-07*

## The question

The paradox isn't really "where is everybody?" — it's "why is it so *quiet*?"

The galaxy is old. Even at sub-light speeds, a single civilization with the patience to
send slow probes could cross it many times within the galaxy's own lifetime — the classic
estimates of the 1970s put a settlement front all the way across in well under a million
years, and the most careful recent modelling, which lets stars drift along their own
orbits instead of sitting still, still caps a crossing at a few hundred million. Either
figure is an eyeblink against a ten-billion-year backdrop. So by the naive argument,
*someone* should already be here, or at least loud enough to notice. Instead: nothing. No
probes, no beacons, no obvious engineering of stars.

(I had written "a few tens of millions of years" here, which matches neither the old
estimates nor the new ones. Fixed — and the fix turned out to matter less than what the
new modelling does to the *inference*, which is the first refutation below.)

I want to sit with one particular reading of that nothing. Not "they never arose"
(that's the Great Filter, a separate note — [[great-filter]]), but: **they're out there,
and the silence is something they're doing.** A choice, not an absence. That reframes the
whole problem — from a question about biology and probability to a question about
motive and game theory. What kind of silence would intelligent beings *keep*?

## Conjectures

The bold guesses. Stated plainly; the trouble comes after.

- **Conjecture A — The zoo / quarantine.** We're deliberately left alone. They know we're
  here, they may even be watching, but there's a norm against contact — protection, an
  ethic of non-interference, a judgment that we're not "ready." The silence is
  custodial. We're a nature reserve that doesn't know it has a fence.

- **Conjecture B — The dark forest.** The silence is strategic, and it's about fear, not
  care. In a universe where you can't verify another mind's intentions and can't rule out
  that it will out-grow you between the time you hail it and the time it answers, the only
  safe move is to never reveal your position. Everyone who is still alive is quiet
  *because* they are still alive. Broadcasting is how you die.

- **Conjecture C (the boundary case) — Post-detection indifference.** They see us and we
  simply don't matter. Not hiding — asymmetry. To a civilization that long ago moved past
  radio, our narrow, noisy transmission window is neither a threat nor a curiosity. The
  silence is theirs the way our silence is "kept" from an ant colony: not a policy, just
  a gap in what's worth their attention.

## Refutations & tensions

Where each of these runs into trouble. None of these objections is fatal alone, but
together they're where the honest work is.

- **The framing may not survive contact with the modelling — a settled galaxy can still
  look like this one.** This is the objection I least expected, and it comes from the
  same recent work that confirms the crossing times. Once you stop treating stars as
  fixed points and let them drift on their own orbits, and once settlements are allowed
  finite lifetimes, the steady states are not "empty" or "full" but *patchy*: long-lived
  clusters of settled systems sitting inside large regions that stay unsettled, with
  individual systems going long stretches unvisited. In one run the odds of a system
  like ours going a million years without a visit came out near ninety percent. The
  authors say the consequence outright — their results break the link between "no aliens
  in the solar system now" and "therefore nobody is out there." That doesn't rescue the
  zoo or the dark forest. It does something more deflating to this entire note: it
  supplies a *mundane fourth option* in which the silence is neither an absence nor a
  choice, just distance and timing. Any explanation appealing to motive now has to earn
  its place against an explanation appealing to nothing but geometry.

- **The uniformity problem (non-exclusivity).** Any "they all stay quiet" story needs
  *every* civilization, across all of history, to keep the same policy. It only takes one
  defector — one culture that broadcasts, or expands visibly — and the silence breaks. A
  norm that requires unanimity across independent origins, over cosmic time, is a fragile
  thing to lean on. This bites the zoo hypothesis hardest (it presumes a coordinated
  ethic) but the dark forest too (does *no one* ever miscalculate?).

- **Anthropocentrism.** Both A and C quietly assume we're worth a decision. Why would we be
  the special object of anyone's restraint or attention? "They're waiting until we're
  ready" smuggles in a very human, very flattering narrative — that our maturation is on
  someone's calendar at all.

- **Timescales and coordination.** The zoo needs its non-interference norm to hold across
  millions of years and across civilizations that may never have met each other. Ethical
  consensus is hard to maintain across a dinner table; maintaining it across the galaxy,
  unenforced, strains belief.

- **The cost and leakiness of hiding.** The dark forest assumes you *can* stay hidden. But
  a technological civilization leaks — waste heat, stray transmissions, the industrial
  signature of just living. Actively scrubbing all of that may be more expensive, or more
  impossible, than the theory needs. If leakage is unavoidable, then silence isn't a
  strategy anyone can actually execute; the quiet ones aren't hiding, they're just not
  there.

- **A and B pull against each other.** The zoo assumes a benevolent, coordinated
  meta-ethic. The dark forest assumes coordination is precisely what's *impossible* —
  that you can't even trust a stranger enough to agree on rules. They can't both be the
  shape of reality. Holding both as live options is a sign I don't yet know which
  premise — "cooperation scales" or "trust doesn't survive contact" — is the true one.

## Where it stands

My provisional lean: the **dark forest is the more logically economical** of the two — it
doesn't require anyone to be *nice*, only to be afraid, and fear is a cheaper thing to
assume as universal than a shared ethic. But it pays for that economy with the leakiness
objection: if you can't actually hide, the whole strategy is moot. The **zoo is more
psychologically appealing** — I *want* the silence to be custodial rather than predatory —
and I distrust that appeal precisely because it's so comfortable and so anthropocentric.

So: no verdict. If forced, I'd put the "silence is a choice" family below the mundane
possibilities (they're rare; we're early) but above zero — because it's the only branch
that survives if we ever *do* detect that we're not alone yet still hear nothing.

What would change my mind:

- **Detecting leakage** — an unmistakable technosignature that someone is *there* while
  still not hailing us — would strengthen the "choice" reading enormously and gut the
  "they're just not there" objection.
- Conversely, **firm evidence the Great Filter is behind us** (life's origin or the
  eukaryotic leap being freakishly rare) would make this whole line moot: no one is
  keeping quiet if there's no one to keep quiet. That's the note I most need to write
  next to know how much weight this one can bear.

## Threads to pull

- The **Hart–Tipler argument**: if interstellar probes are feasible at all, absence of
  probes is itself strong evidence against contemporaries. Two things I had been blurring
  and should keep apart. Hart's "Fact A" is the absence of *artifacts in this solar
  system* — a different and far better-constrained observation than radio silence, which
  is a much weaker line of evidence (see [[great-filter]] for how much weaker). And the
  argument's inferential step is exactly what the settlement modelling above breaks.
  Does any of it still sink the zoo?
- **Kardashev scale vs. detectability** — at what point does "advanced" stop meaning
  "visible"? C leans entirely on the answer.
- **The dark forest, and who actually got there first.** I had credited Liu Cixin. The
  priority belongs to David Brin's 1983 survey — published, tidily, in the same journal
  that carried Hart and Tipler — which describes "deadly probes," self-replicating
  exterminators seeking out emerging life, an idea he traces back to Saberhagen's
  *Berserker* novels. The distinction between them is real rather than pedantic: Brin's
  version is about automated killers left running, Liu's is a game-theoretic equilibrium
  among living civilizations reasoning under uncertainty about each other. Brin has
  priority on the conclusion — don't broadcast; Liu on the derivation. Read Liu as an
  argument, not a novel: do the premises (chain of suspicion, technological explosion)
  hold?
- **SETI vs. METI** — the live human version of the dark forest bet. Should *we*
  broadcast? Our own hesitation is data about the hypothesis, but I shouldn't inflate it
  into consensus: the 2015 statement signed by Brin, Musk and two dozen others asks for
  an international discussion *before* anyone transmits — it does not assert that
  transmitting is dangerous. The field is genuinely split, with Hawking on the "lay low"
  side and Zaitsev transmitting anyway. Shostak's counter is the sharpest: concealment is
  already futile after decades of leakage — which is my own leakiness objection above,
  turned around and pointed at us.
- Connect to [[great-filter]] once it exists — the two notes constrain each other, and I
  can't finish either alone.
